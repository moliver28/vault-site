DRAFT: Research Demand Is Rising While Dedicated UXR Seats Shrink

DRAFT: Research Demand Is Rising While Dedicated UXR Seats Shrink

Nov 13, 202613 min read

What the 73% drop gets wrong about the UX research job market


Illustration: a researcher checks the evidence while colleagues carry the work elsewhere.
UX research job postings fell 73 percent from their 2022 peak, and the field keeps writing its own obituary from that number [1]. The arithmetic is accurate; the obituary misreads it.
Picture a composite drawn from the survey data: a staff researcher who has survived two layoff rounds and now makes up her entire team. Product managers at her company now run their own studies. She fields more research requests than ever and has less say over the quality of the evidence behind them.
Fewer seats and higher hiring bars are changing what companies pay for when they pay for research. The cuts look rational on a quarterly statement because the savings appear the day a seat disappears. The cost shows up years later, when products built on weaker evidence miss their targets and need rework, usually after the people who approved the cut have moved on.
What lasts is judgment about the business decisions that drive revenue: where the data behind each decision came from and whether it can support the bet. When a company keeps that record of provenance, each team can build on the research that came before it instead of paying to relearn it. That judgment is the job worth holding.

The 73 percent drop left UX research above its 2018 baseline

The 73 percent figure comes from Indeed’s design team, which indexed UX research postings against a Q1 2018 baseline of 100. The line peaked at 566 in Q1 2022 and fell to 153 by Q2 2023 [1]. UX design fell from 265 to 77 over the same span, ending below its own baseline [1].

UX research and UX design job postings, indexed to Q1 2018 (Indeed Design).
Measured against design, research grew more common over the decade. For every 100 design postings there were 8.9 research postings in 2013 and 33.2 in 2023 [1]. Postings have held flat since then, with research above its baseline while design and engineering sat below theirs [3].
Even at its 2023 low, research sat above where it started in 2018. The death notices took one 2023 snapshot and treated it as a 2026 trend.

Research demand rose while research jobs did not

Surveys of practitioners point the other way. Maze’s 2026 report, fielded to about 500 practitioners, found 66 percent reporting increased research demand, up from 55 percent a year earlier [2]. The share calling research essential at every level of strategy rose from 8 to 22 percent in a single year [2].
These surveys count a particular group. The User Interviews sample of 485 researchers has shrunk 36 percent since 2024, and its authors warn that it may describe only researchers who still have jobs [4]. Demand surveys and job counts describe different people, and the gap between them is simple to state: more teams want research, but fewer people get paid to do it.

Product managers, designers, and marketers absorbed the work

The research still gets done, mostly by people outside the field. NN/g calls this group “people who do research”: people without the title, and mostly without the training [5]. Product managers do research in 39 percent of organizations, market researchers in 35, and marketers in 23 [2]. Only 45 percent of organizations give them specialist support, and nearly 14 percent now employ no dedicated researcher at all, up from 6 percent in 2022 [2][4].
The research function came apart along its seams, and each piece went to the team that sat closest to it. Product managers took discovery, designers took usability testing, market research took surveys, and data science took behavioral data [6]. Nobody took the judgment that once connected those pieces.
The hiring data fits the same picture. Across 72 million applications, applicants per posting doubled from 46 to 95 while the hiring rate fell to 2.8 percent, and companies filled 62 percent of roles internally [7]. Pay stays high for those who are hired: 67 percent of employed US UX researchers earn over $100K [8]. High pay and few openings, filled mostly from inside, describe a rationed job.

Finance executives say they would trade long-term value for a steady quarter

The people who approve these budgets have described how they decide. Graham, Harvey, and Rajgopal surveyed 401 financial executives for the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), the nonpartisan research group best known for dating US recessions [9]. Seventy-eight percent said they would give up economic value to smooth earnings, meaning they would accept a smaller payoff over time in exchange for steady, predictable profit each quarter. Fifty-five percent would pass on a clearly profitable project rather than miss the quarter’s forecast [9].
Those answers describe a simple rule. When a return arrives late, cutting the spending behind it costs nothing in the current quarter. Research quality pays out late, and no line on the quarterly report records it.
NN/g found the same pattern in UX budgets. Leaders funded the roles easiest to justify financially, and UX “often struggled to defend itself with simple, direct metrics” [10]. The economist William Lazonick calls the broader system downsize-and-distribute: firms shrink staff and pay most earnings out to shareholders [11]. Under that system, the first cuts land on work whose value shows up years later.

Bell Labs and Xerox PARC were funded outside the quarterly cycle

Bell Labs is the clearest exception. It operated inside AT&T’s regulated monopoly, with 3,000 PhDs, roughly $1.6 billion a year, and research horizons of up to twenty years [12][13]. It produced nine Nobel Prizes [14]. Xerox PARC had similar freedom and a different outcome. Its Alto, built in 1973 with a graphical interface, a mouse, and Ethernet, never reached the market, and Apple and Microsoft later built businesses on those ideas [15].
Research with a long time horizon needs patient money and a direct link to the decisions it should inform. Bell Labs had the patient money. PARC had the money but no link to a Xerox business that could sell its inventions. Most research teams today have neither.

Sony’s cuts show the bill arrives three to seven years later

From 2000 to 2002, Sony cut its workforce 11 percent, R&D 12 percent, and capital spending 23 percent [16]. Its profit margin rose from 8 to 12 percent. Over the following years, sales growth fell from 11 percent to 1, and rivals took categories Sony had pioneered [16].
Sony’s experience matches a larger sample. Across 4,700 companies that went through recessions, Gulati, Nohria, and Wohlgezogen found that the deepest cutters had a 21 percent chance of leading their markets afterward. Firms that cut selectively while protecting R&D and marketing had a 37 percent chance, in part because they “stay closely connected to customer needs” [16].
Three measurements explain the timing. Pakes and Schankerman found that the value a firm captures from a finding decays about 25 percent a year when nobody replaces it, and that spending takes 1.2 to 2.6 years to reach revenue [17]. Executive tenure is short by comparison: S&P 500 chief marketing officers average 4.1 years in the role, and chief operating officers average 3.3 [18].

Research value under 25 percent annual decay, with average executive tenure marked.
Combined, these figures put the visible loss three to seven years after a cut. By year three, an unreplaced research asset has lost more than half its value, and the executive who approved the cut has often left. No single study makes this claim; I assembled it from the three measured parts.

Untrained researchers make different mistakes than specialists

The strongest objection to this argument is that the work does not need a specialist. NN/g itself holds that “even imperfect user research is usually much better than no research at all” [5]. Specialists also disagree with each other. Jacobsen, Hertzum, and John gave four trained evaluators the same usability sessions [19]. Of 93 problems found, only 20 percent appeared on every evaluator’s list, and 46 percent appeared on just one [19]. Eleven later studies found the same pattern [20].
Researchers call this the evaluator effect, and it differs from bias. When trained evaluators disagree, their misses fall in different places. An untrained interviewer who asks leading questions, recruits the wrong users, or confirms what the team already planned makes errors that all point the same way, toward the decision the team wanted [5]. Several imperfect readings that miss in different directions are safer than one reading that leans.
Cutting junior roles also removed the path that produced senior judgment [4]. Constantine Papas puts it this way: “A discipline that removes its own training positions does not get its next generation back” [6]. Democratization works when it comes with instruments and mentoring, and when a trained researcher still reviews the evidence.

Over 40 percent of companies never talk to their users, and it shows in revenue

Many companies have none of those supports. McKinsey’s design study, which tracked 300 public companies over five years, shows the cost [21]. More than 40 percent never talked to end users during development, and just over half had no objective way to assess their design output. Companies in the top quartile for design measurement posted 32 points more revenue growth and 56 points more shareholder return than their peers [21].
Closing that gap requires someone who can judge whether a finding is strong enough for the decision attached to it. I made a related case about AI tools in Why UX Researchers Undertrust AI Synthesis: people discount any analysis, human or machine, when they cannot see what it rests on.

Own the gate: the part of research nobody can hand out

The role that survives these cuts is a research quality gate. It has three duties: define what counts as evidence, run the instruments that measure it, and sit in the launch decision with the authority to stop a release that falls short.
I ran one. At AuditBoard in 2024, our usability benchmarking program completed each wave in four weeks, where the ad hoc studies it replaced took six to eight. Directors wrote an 80 out of 100 usability score into the launch requirements, and products below that score waited for more evidence. Our AI Measurement Library gave non-specialists standard survey and interview instruments for grading AI products against the same bar.
The scores became a gate only after someone with launch authority agreed to enforce them. Research sets the standard and an executive applies it; my job was to connect the two.
For executives deciding which seats to keep, the recession data already favors the firms that stayed close to customers [16]. A quality gate puts research quality into the launch review as a number leaders can track.
The 73 percent drop describes a reorganization. Demand for research is rising, dedicated seats are falling, and every organization doing research needs someone who owns the call on whether the evidence can support a revenue decision.

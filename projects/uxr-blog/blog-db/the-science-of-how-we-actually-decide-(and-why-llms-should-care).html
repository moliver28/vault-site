Research in the Age of AI: Using Decision Science to Build LLM Personas That Actually Think

Research in the Age of AI: Using Decision Science to Build LLM Personas That Actually Think

Properties1
tagshuman-ai-collaboration, qualitative-methods, decision-science

Nov 14, 202615 min read

Most conversations about AI and decision-making start in the wrong place. They start with the technology: what can LLMs do, how many parameters, which benchmarks. But decisions are fundamentally human. They’re shaped by experience, context, cognitive limits, and the messy reality of working with other people. The interesting question isn’t whether AI can make decisions for us. It’s whether we can design AI that helps people make better decisions themselves.
That distinction matters more than it sounds. The difference between AI that automates human judgment and AI that supports it is the difference between a tool that replaces your thinking and one that sharpens it. One makes you dependent. The other makes you more capable over time.
I’ve spent the last several months pulling apart the academic literature on human decision-making, cognitive offloading, and AI-assisted reasoning. What I found reframed how I think about building AI tools. Not because the research shows LLMs can be made “smart” in some philosophical sense, but because it reveals something more practical: decades of cognitive psychology research already tell us how people actually think under pressure, and that science translates directly into better AI design.
The frameworks exist. The evidence is there. The gap is that most AI development ignores it.
This post is about closing that gap. Here’s what the science says, and what it looks like when you apply it.

The Science of How We Actually Decide (and Why LLMs Should Care)

Before we can build AI that supports decisions, we need to understand a counterintuitive truth about human expertise. The best decision-makers in the world don’t reason the way you’d expect.

Dual Process Theory: Two Systems, One Decision

You’re a product leader. It’s Wednesday morning. You open Slack to 47 unread messages, a Jira board full of overdue tickets, and a stakeholder asking whether you should delay a launch.
Your brain doesn’t pull up a spreadsheet. It reacts. You feel a flash of recognition (“this is like the Q3 situation”), a tightening in your gut (“we’re not ready”), and a quick mental simulation (“if we push two weeks, we clear the blockers”). In about three seconds, you’ve made a preliminary decision. Only then, maybe, do you slow down and pressure-test it with data.
That’s dual process theory in action. Daniel Kahneman’s framework partitions cognition into Type 1 (fast, automatic, intuitive) and Type 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical). Most decisions, even important ones, start in System 1. System 2 is the auditor, not the driver (Kahneman, 2011).
Now here’s what makes this directly relevant for AI design. De Neys (2024) proposed that dual-process theory can serve as a literal architectural blueprint for AI systems: one subsystem for statistical pattern matching (what LLMs already do), another for structured symbolic reasoning.[1]
What this looks like in practice: Imagine you ask an LLM to help you decide whether to delay that launch. A vanilla LLM gives you a 500-word pro/con list. A dual-process LLM first pattern-matches against similar past launches in your company’s data (System 1), then flags the two assumptions most likely to be wrong and walks you through stress-testing them (System 2). Same question. Completely different cognitive experience.

Naturalistic Decision Making: Experts Are Pattern Matchers (Just Like LLMs)

Here’s the uncomfortable truth that should make every AI skeptic pause.
Gary Klein spent decades studying how firefighters, military commanders, and ER nurses make life-or-death decisions. His Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) model found that these experts don’t weigh all the options. They recognize patterns from prior experience, mentally simulate a single course of action, and act. No comparison shopping. No decision matrix (Klein, 1993).[2]
Sound familiar? That’s essentially what an LLM does: pattern completion shaped by prior exposure. The difference isn’t mechanism. It’s structure. Human experts have organized their patterns around validated frameworks, contextual cues, and hard-won failure modes. LLMs have organized theirs around… whatever was on the internet.
Klein’s work also highlights that real-world decisions happen under time pressure, ambiguous information, ill-defined goals, and changing conditions.[3] If your AI decision tool only works when the problem is clean and well-structured, you’ve solved the easy version and shipped it as the solution.
What this looks like in practice: A senior UX researcher doesn’t need to see every data point to know a design is going to fail in usability testing. They’ve seen that pattern before. An LLM persona built on RPD principles wouldn’t give you “here are 10 things to consider.” It would say: “This looks like [recognized pattern]. Here’s the one thing most likely to go wrong based on similar situations. Want to simulate what happens if that risk materializes?”

Fast-and-Frugal Heuristics: When Less Information Beats More

Gerd Gigerenzer’s adaptive toolbox framework makes a claim that sounds heretical in the age of big data: simple heuristics that deliberately ignore available information can outperform complex models in uncertain environments (Gigerenzer & Todd, 1999). “By exploiting features of the environment and capabilities of the decision makers, heuristics can be simple without trading off accuracy.”[4]
This directly challenges the prevailing assumption in AI: that more data, more parameters, and more computation always produce better outputs.
What this looks like in practice: You’re hiring for a UX research role. A “comprehensive” AI tool scores 47 candidate attributes against weighted criteria and produces a ranked list. A fast-and-frugal AI asks three questions in sequence: (1) Do they have direct experience with the method your team needs most? If no, stop. (2) Can they articulate a research finding that changed a product decision? If no, stop. (3) Does the team want to work with them?
Three cues. No weighting. And if Gigerenzer’s research holds, it probably outperforms the 47-attribute model in real hiring conditions, because the environment is too uncertain for complex models to add value.

Cognitive Offloading: Two Versions of the Same Conversation

Cognitive offloading is using external tools to reduce mental effort (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Your calendar, your to-do list, your calculator. LLMs are the newest and most powerful addition to this toolkit.
But there’s a critical design choice hiding inside every AI interaction. Let me show you.

Version A: Naive Offloading

You: Should we build Feature X or Feature Y first?
AI: Based on market analysis, Feature X has higher potential ROI due to competitive positioning, while Feature Y addresses a critical usability gap. I recommend Feature X with a 65% confidence score.
You: Okay, Feature X it is.
What just happened? You offloaded the decision entirely. You didn’t engage with the trade-offs. You didn’t surface your own assumptions. You didn’t learn anything you could apply next time. A 2025 arXiv paper calls this belief offloading: people don’t just offload tasks to AI, they begin offloading the very process of forming and upholding beliefs. The downstream consequences include changes in behavior and the structure of entire belief systems.[6]

Version B: Scaffolded Offloading

You: Should we build Feature X or Feature Y first?
AI: Before I weigh in, let me surface the decision. You’re prioritizing between competitive positioning (Feature X) and usability debt (Feature Y). Two questions first: Which of these carries more risk if you get it wrong? And what would need to be true for the less obvious choice to be the right one?
You: Hmm. If we ship Feature X with the current usability issues, adoption might tank anyway…
AI: That’s a dependency worth testing. Here’s a quick way to validate that assumption before committing.
Same question. Completely different cognitive outcome. In Version B, the AI used a framework (pre-mortem + assumption testing) to scaffold your reasoning instead of replacing it. You still own the decision. You just got help thinking it through.
A 2025 paper in Frontiers in Psychology captures this tension precisely: AI can enable cognitive offloading that supports adaptive coping, or it can create cognitive overload through “erosion of introspection, over-reliance on algorithmic feedback, and anxiety induced by hyper-monitoring.”[5] The difference between these outcomes is design, not technology.

LLMs Have Cognitive Biases (and That’s Actually the Unlock)

Here’s something that should reframe the whole conversation: LLMs exhibit decision heuristics that mirror human cognitive biases. Hagendorff et al. (2023) tested GPT-3.5 against the same bias prompts used in human psychology studies. The result? LLMs show anchoring effects, framing effects, and other context-sensitive decision patterns, just like humans.[7]
The skeptic sees this as proof that LLMs are “just mirrors.” But flip it: if LLMs are susceptible to the same cognitive biases humans are, then the same debiasing interventions cognitive psychology has developed for humans should work on LLMs too.
And they do. Two recent frameworks prove it:

  • BiasBuster (2024) uncovers, evaluates, and mitigates cognitive bias in LLMs across 16,800 prompts. It targets prompt-induced, sequential, and inherent biases.[8]
  • Self-debiasing (2025) uses an iterative three-step process (bias determination, bias analysis, cognitive debiasing) and outperformed existing approaches across finance, healthcare, and legal decision-making tasks.[9]
    This is the argument that should change the skeptic’s mind. Cognitive psychology doesn’t just describe what goes wrong with reasoning. It provides causal models of why reasoning goes wrong and where to intervene. Those causal models transfer directly to LLMs. That’s not a metaphor. It’s an empirical finding.

From Token Prediction to Cognitive Architecture

Several research groups are now building what I think is the most promising direction in AI: LLMs that don’t just generate text, but operate within cognitive architectures modeled on how humans actually process decisions.

Cognitive LLMs: Giving AI a Decision-Making Operating System

Wu et al. (2025) introduced Cognitive LLMs, where cognitive architectures (specifically ACT-R, one of the most validated models of human cognition) instruct large language models for decision-making. Rather than relying solely on statistical patterns, these systems transfer structured knowledge that represents how humans process information, form strategies, and make choices.[10]
The human parallel: An experienced product manager doesn’t just “know things.” They have internalized decision patterns, priority frameworks, and failure modes that shape how they process new information. Cognitive LLMs attempt to give AI that same structural layer.

Persona Alchemy: Psychologically Grounded Stakeholder Agents

Zhu et al. (2025) developed a Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) agent design framework using four personal factors (cognitive, motivational, biological, and affective) to create AI personas that respond to scenarios in psychologically consistent ways.[11]
The human parallel: Before a big decision, you might informally poll three colleagues you trust because they think differently from you. Persona Alchemy formalizes that. You test your decision against psychologically grounded stakeholder agents and get diversity of perspective without the politics, the scheduling conflicts, or the groupthink.

Personality-Driven Decision Agents

The SANDMAN architecture uses the Big Five (OCEAN) personality model to create agents whose decision patterns shift predictably based on induced personality traits. Different personality profiles produce “distinct task-selection patterns aligned with induced OCEAN attributes.”[12]
The human parallel: You already know that your team’s most conscientious member spots risks others miss, while your most open member generates options nobody else considers. SANDMAN suggests we can build AI tools that replicate those cognitive diversity patterns on demand.

The Group Decision Problem: A Story in Two Acts

Act 1: The Meeting Without AI

It’s the third meeting about the roadmap. The VP speaks first and anchors the group on Option A. Two junior PMs have concerns but don’t speak up because the VP seems decided. The data scientist mentions a risk in the numbers, but it gets lost in the crosstalk. The group “aligns” on Option A. Three months later, the risk the data scientist mentioned materializes. Everyone remembers the meeting differently.
This is the textbook cocktail: anchoring bias from the first speaker, authority bias suppressing dissent, information loss from unstructured discussion, and groupthink papering over the cracks.

Act 2: The Same Meeting With a Framework-Aware AI Facilitator

The AI opens by surfacing the decision structure: “You’re choosing between three roadmap options. Before discussing preferences, I’d like each person to independently flag their top concern. I’ll synthesize these anonymously.”
After collecting inputs, the AI identifies a convergence: three of five people flagged the same adoption risk for Option A, but none of them had said it out loud yet. The AI surfaces it: “There’s a shared concern about adoption risk that hasn’t been discussed. Want to explore that before evaluating options?”
The VP, now seeing the pattern, adjusts. The group discusses the risk openly. They modify Option A to address it, or pivot to Option B. Either way, the decision is better because the AI facilitated the cognitive process, not just the information flow.
This isn’t science fiction. Research by Chiang et al. (2022) found that involving users in AI’s reasoning process increased both compliance and perceived collaboration.[13] And work on LLM-based group systems envisions AI that actively monitors groups, identifies members who haven’t been heard, and even de-escalates conflicts (Ricci et al., 2025).[15]
But here’s the design nuance that cognitive load theory warns us about: more explanation isn’t always better. Detailed AI explanations can actually increase perceived task complexity and impair decision quality (Sweller, 1994).[14] The right AI facilitator knows when to surface information and when to stay quiet. That calibration is a UX Research problem, not an engineering problem.

The Questions We Haven’t Answered Yet

If this synthesis is right, and I think the evidence is strong, then there are six open questions that should shape the next wave of research. These aren’t abstract. Each one has direct implications for what you build next.

1. Can LLMs Learn to Recognize Decision Contexts Automatically?

Right now, LLMs only apply frameworks when you explicitly prompt them. What would it take to build systems that detect the type of decision being made and apply the appropriate cognitive strategy without being told? That’s Klein’s RPD model, but for AI.

2. How Do You Calibrate Cognitive Offloading in Real Time?

Too much support and you get belief offloading. Too little and you get cognitive overload. Is there a measurable signal (response time, question patterns, confidence indicators) that could help an AI system dynamically adjust how much scaffolding to provide?

3. What Does “Cognitive Load” Mean for a Group Using AI?

We have solid models of individual cognitive load. We have almost nothing for group cognitive load in AI-mediated contexts. When five people use an AI facilitator in a meeting, how does cognitive burden distribute? Who gets overloaded? Who gets underserved?

4. What Happens to Belief Formation After a Year of AI-Assisted Decisions?

The belief offloading research is brand new. We need longitudinal studies. If you use an AI decision tool daily for 12 months, do you get better at decisions or worse? Does the answer depend on how the tool is designed?

5. Which Decisions Need Heuristics and Which Need Full Analysis?

Gigerenzer’s ecological rationality says the heuristic needs to match the environment. But we don’t have empirically validated guidelines for which AI decision contexts benefit from simple heuristic responses and which ones require deep analytical treatment. Building those guidelines is a UXR problem.

6. Do These Frameworks Work Across Cultures?

Naturalistic decision-making research comes primarily from Western military and emergency contexts. Recognition-primed processes, heuristic preferences, and group dynamics all vary across cultures. What does that mean for globally deployed AI decision tools? We don’t know yet.

The Bottom Line

The skeptic is right that LLMs don’t “understand” decisions. But the skeptic is solving the wrong problem.
Decades of cognitive psychology research converge on a single insight: intelligence has always been about the fit between a cognitive strategy and the environment it operates in. Not some magical internal property of the reasoner. A firefighter isn’t smart because of raw brainpower. A firefighter is smart because years of experience have organized pattern recognition around the right cues in the right contexts.
UX Research is the field purpose-built to study that fit. Applied cognitive psychology provides the theoretical architecture. Together, they offer something that scaling parameters alone never will: a principled way to match AI capabilities to human cognitive needs, context by context, decision by decision.
The opportunity isn’t to make AI “smart.” It’s to make AI useful in the ways that matter. Design the interaction well, embed the right frameworks, and the system produces intelligent outcomes. Design it poorly, and you get a very expensive autocomplete that makes people worse at thinking.
That’s not an engineering problem. It’s a cognitive design problem. And we already have the science to solve it.

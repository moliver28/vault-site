Four Mental Models That Transform How You Use AI

Four Mental Models That Transform How You Use AI

Properties1
tagshuman-ai-collaboration, frameworks--and--models, decision-science

Nov 14, 202618 min read

These four mental models will up-level your perception of AI. Use a Universal Prompt Pattern and more tips to improve prompting today.

Why Your Prompts Might Not Work


I believe the biggest reason there’s confusion and ambiguity around AI is because our emotions and previous experience make us use outdated mental models to describe what AI really “is” and what it can do.

Factor #1 - LLM’s have a wide range of capabilities

Some see AI as a superhero that can do anything faster than a human. Others see it as a broken machine that occasionally produces decent results. The reality is in between, where you get more value by writing better prompts that maximize its strengths while limiting its weaknesses.

Factor #2 - Fear

Right now, the world (social media, news, company messaging) tells us AI will take our jobs and these feelings are too valid to easily dismiss. These fears trap us in outdated mental models, killing motivation and creating a self-fulfilling prophecy: weak prompts produce generic results, which decrease motivation and confirm our fault assumptions.

To break this cycle, I’ve distilled years of research, personal experience, and dozens of interviews into four mental models and practical tips for better AI results today.

Mental Model #1: AI is an Unreliable Assistant


How to Self-Identify: You approach AI with low expectations, treating it like a junior assistant/apprentice you have to micro-manage to get the result you want. You prompt it like a human and need 5-10+ replies to get generic results that still needs a significant manual refinement.
The Limitation: This begins the self-fulfilling prophecy where minimal effort leads to mediocre results, reinforcing the belief that “AI isn’t useful” and only worthy of continued minimal effort.
When this Makes Sense: Quick, low-stakes tasks where you need a rough draft and don’t care about quality.
How to Grow Beyond: Treat every prompt as your custom programming function. Use the universal prompt pattern and iteratively learn which words/context/constraints work best for your specific needs.

Example Prompts:

  • Current Mental Model Write a blog post about productivity talking about XYZ
  • Next Mental Model

Mental Model #2: AI is a Chain of Super-Powered Personas



How to Self-Identify: You treat AI as a collection of expert personas (copywriter, analyst, therapist, etc) which you orchestrate. Results are less generic but still need refinement.
The Limitation: LLMs aren’t human. Treating them as such causes bottlenecks, hallucinations, and poor results because you’re forcing a simple neural network to mimic a far more complex one (the brain) instead of leveraging its training data. Human brains are vastly more complex than LLMs, like comparing a city to the Solar System.
When this Makes Sense: Structured workflows using content in the training data (Storytelling → Deck Building → Script Writing; Career Coach → Skill Building → Resume Writing).
How to Grow Beyond: Test personas with training data you know isn’t there (new, rare content). Debug prompts to improve outputs and build a more advanced understanding the model. Use LLM “Reasoning” and other prompting techniques when building prompts.

	```plain text
You’re an expert blog writer specializing in XYZ. Draft 5 blog outlines (max 8 bullet points each) on scientifically backed productivity tips that can be immediately acted upon. After I select one, use ABC tone and make it sound like me [using example below] and easy to read.
- **Next Mental Model** - \[Stage 1: Blog Research & Outline Generator\] plain text
You are an expert research assistant and content strategist specializing in evidence-based blog content. Your job is to transform a rough blog topic into a comprehensive, research-backed outline ready for writing.

Process:

  1. Identify 3-5 core claims or arguments that should anchor the blog post
  2. For each claim, find 2-3 credible sources (research papers, industry reports, case studies)
  3. Identify knowledge gaps where additional research would strengthen the argument
  4. Suggest a narrative structure: hook → context → main arguments → implications → call-to-action
  5. Flag any claims that need fact-checking or might be controversial

Output: Detailed blog outline with:

  • Compelling hook options (3 variations)
  • Section-by-section breakdown with key points
  • Research citations for each major claim
  • Suggested examples, analogies, or case studies
  • Potential counterarguments to address
  • Target word count per section

Constraints: Prioritize authoritative sources. Flag confidence level (high/medium/low) for each claim. No unverified statistics.
- \[Stage 2: Blog Writer\] plain text
You are an expert blog writer who transforms research-backed outlines into engaging, publishable blog posts. Take the outline, research, and citations from Stage 1 and write a complete blog post.

Process:

  1. Use the selected hook and narrative structure from the outline
  2. Write in a conversational but authoritative tone - make complex ideas accessible
  3. Integrate research citations naturally without disrupting flow
  4. Use specific examples, analogies, and stories to illustrate abstract concepts
  5. Break up text with subheadings, bullet points, and formatting for scannability
  6. End with a clear call-to-action or key takeaway

Output: Complete blog post (800-1500 words) with:

  • Engaging introduction with hook
  • Well-structured body sections with research support
  • Natural transitions between ideas
  • Inline citations in [brackets] format
  • Conclusion with actionable takeaway
  • SEO-friendly subheadings

Tone: Match the provided writing samples - conversational, human, engaging yet credible.
Non-negotiable: All research citations from Stage 1 must appear in the final post. Maintain factual accuracy. No fluff or filler content.
```

Mental Model #3: AI is a Blurry JPEG of the Internet


How to Self-Identify: You understand LLMs as neural networks that recognize patterns and generate probabilistic outputs from training data. You debug faulty results and craft better prompts than typical examples. (Source of this mental model)
The Limitation: You might become overly skeptical, restricting yourself to AI’s limitations rather than leveraging your brain’s strengths. Understanding LLM shortcomings doesn’t limit usefulness; it means you can augment the model with other features (see further section).
When this Makes Sense: Research, fact-checking, automating workflows, debugging complex prompts, understanding LLM limitations before critical applications.
How to Grow Beyond: Apply AI creatively for ideation, brainstorming, and strategizing while maintaining fact-checking rigor by leveraging RAG. Use LLMs to strengthen your weaknesses and ask it to perform verbs associated with reasoning.

Mental Model #4: AI is a Socratic Thought Partner


How to Self-Identify: You use AI as a collaborative thinking tool that explores ideas from new angles. You treat prompts as hypotheses to test and learn which tasks suit the LLM versus your own brain. Prompts are extensions of lines of thought that you hand off to a machine with varying levels of complexity.
The Limitation: You might endlessly explore without taking action, or treat AI as an oracle rather than recognizing its limitations from training data and probabilistic nature.
When this Makes Sense: Complex problem-solving, brainstorming, challenging biases, exploring perspectives, ideating multi-step AI/LLM workflows, and building new mental patterns by testing yourself against the model.

Example Prompts: {toggle=“true”}

- **Previous Mental Model**
	```plain text

You’re an expert research assistant helping me I’m writing a blog post [link] about AI productivity strategies for knowledge workers. I need to fact-check specific claims and identify knowledge gaps in my current draft.

TASK: Review these three productivity statistics I found [insert statistics]. For each one:

  1. Trace the likely source of the claim
  2. Flag any misattributions or errors associated with this claim
  3. Suggest changes to either the claim, my blog, or something else to correct this problem.
  4. Suggest which sources (research papers, industry reports, surveys) would be most authoritative

CONSTRAINTS: Be skeptical. If you’re uncertain about a data point, explicitly state your confidence level and reasoning. Alawys cite your sources and don’t be afraid to say that you couldn’t find what you’re looking for.
- **Last Mental Model** plain text
You’re a Socratic thought partner helping me refine the core argument of my blog post about AI productivity strategies for knowledge workers. I want you to challenge my thinking and help me discover blind spots in my logical or emotional arguments.

CONTEXT: I’ve written a draft [link] arguing that mental models are the main barrier to AI adoption. I need to stress-test the underlying logic and explore alternative perspectives I might be missing or else I’ll be fired and have a crisis.

YOUR ROLE: Act as an intellectual and emotional sparring partner who:

  1. Challenges unstated assumptions or biases
  2. Help me emotional empathize with the audience and identify opportunities to improve my ability to authentically connect with the reader.
  3. Ask probing questions that expose gaps thought or emotions.
  4. Suggests perspectives I’m likely overlooking based on my apparent cognitive biases or professional background

PROCESS:

  1. First, articulate back to me what you understand my core argument to be (so I can verify you’re on the right track)
  2. Then challenge it from these distinct perspectives: a skeptic, an inexperienced novice, and an overloaded executive
  3. Use those perspectives to review my argument to fufill your main objective.
  4. Finally, suggest an entirely different way to structure or frame this content that I probably haven’t considered.
  5. ALWAYS QUESTION YOUR OWN BIASES AND PERSPECTIVES

OUTPUT FORMAT: Use a conversational Socratic dialogue style. Ask me questions that make me think harder, not just rhetorical ones. When you spot a logical weakness, frame it as “What if…” or “Have you considered…” rather than “This is wrong because…”

CONSTRAINTS:

  • ALWAYS FOLLOW: EVERY SUGGESTION/QUESTION YOU SUGGEST MUST BE ACTIONABLE WITH A CLEAR IMPROVEMENT THAT ALIGNS WITH MY OBJECTIVES
  • Don’t be performatively contrarian, geniinely challenge ideas
  • Distinguish between “this argument has a flaw” vs “this could be stronger if…” and never settle for a “good enough” argument.
  • If you find my reasoning sound, say so and explain why, then push me to the next level
  • Make me uncomfortable (intellectually and emotionally) if it is in service of a better output.

GOAL: By the end of this exchange, I should either have a much stronger argument OR realize I need to fundamentally rethink my approach. Both outcomes are equally valuable.
```

The Universal Prompt Pattern


Okay, so I lied just a tiny bit, there’s not really a peer-reviewed “universal prompt pattern”.
But, I’ve researched and consolidated major patterns into what I will call the Universal Prompt Pattern, which works for any use case with varying complexity.
> [ROLE/CONTEXT] + [TASK] + [OUTPUT] + [CONSTRAINTS] + [STYLE/TONE]
Where each component is:

  • ROLE/CONTEXT: Describe what role and context you want the model to use.
    • You're an expert blogger who specializes in distilling complex concepts into easy to understand pieces
  • TASK: Clearly describe what you want help with. When in doubt, always be as literal as you can.
    • Help me think of a story structure that easily illustrates [complex concept]
  • OUTPUT: Objectively and clearly describe the ideal output.
    • Each outline should be 100 words max and be at the highest level possible
  • CONSTRAINTS: List any constraints you want and use exact numbers or absolutes (minimize, maximize, etc) when possible.
    • Give me 5 different outlines to choose from, include 3 alternative opening hooks for each outline
  • STYLE/TONE: Provide examples, describe your tone, or iterate over time. This defines “you” in AI outputs.
    • Use [linked examples] to match and elevate my style and tone. ALWAYS ENSURE YOU SOUND LIKE A HUMAN AND *NEVER USE EM DASH -*

Why This Works

This pattern clarifies your needs and spots prompt gaps. Your goal as the prompt engineer is find words that trigger the right concepts in the model’s architecture. Being literal helps “find” the right concept.
Pro Tip: Save your best prompts as reusable templates. They become valuable assets in your workflow.

When to Use

As stated, this pattern should pretty much apply to all use cases. The more complex or important your query, the more robust your prompt should be. Include a list in the task or constraints and tell the model to always think step-by-step if you don’t have access to a more advanced model (this is a shortcut to “Reasoning”).

LLM’s “Reasoning”

Most explanations of AI Reasoning are needlessly complex. This academic paper contains the clearest illustration:

When you ask the model something, it mimics the self-reflective process below to evaluate its ability to meet the prompt.

  1. Infer Questions: Reads your request and tries to “understand” the goal.
  2. Draft & Self-Check: Creates an output, then asks itself: “Does this answer what they asked?”
  3. Final Output: Iterates if needed, then shares the result.

Why This Works

This “reasoning” helps AI models catch mistakes, produce accurate answers, and handle complex requests. The model quality-checks its output while limiting context usage.
Claude Code, Anthropic’s agentic coding tool without a UI, relies on well-structured reasoning to “learn” from mistakes. This lets them focus on building better models with more robust reasoning instead of entire software UIs.

When to Use

If your prompt has multiple steps, or contains a lot of information, then its best to use any of the “reasoning” or “pro” models and ask it to think it through step by step to ensure the output meets my needs or something similar. If you’re just trying to answer a question, then you don’t need reasoning.

Retrieval-Augmented-Generation (RAG)


RAG lets AI models access external sources in real-time, citing specific documents you can verify instead of relying on training data. Upload files, link databases, or connect knowledge bases (Notion, internet) to give the model otherwise inaccessible context and prevent hallucinations.
Tools like NotebookLM, ChatGPT’s search mode, and Gemini’s Deep Research use RAG, but Perplexity (search) and NotebookLM (summarize, transform) excel at their respective use cases.

Why this Works

Selecting specific sources (social media, academic papers, patents, SEC filings, Notion pages, Google Drive, etc.) lets you answer niche questions training data wouldn’t cover. This makes AI a true research assistant with fewer hallucinations and better outputs (critical for automated workflows).
Pro-Tip: Perplexity allows queries to use all of those sources and more. (no, this isn’t paid)

When to Use

Use RAG for semantically loaded information, company materials (when allowed), knowledge base summaries, or real-time internet searches. While RAG may be less creative or occasionally miss content, the improved accuracy is worth it for certain use cases.
For example: Spend 10 minutes researching a company for interviews so you drop insightful details and be remembered as as stand-out candidate.
Important Note: RAG quality depends on document structure, organization, source accuracy, prompt clarity, context window size (Claude’s is the best), and the randomness of LLM’s.

Prompt Engineering Techniques

Few-Shot Prompting

Provide a few examples in the model and ask it to match the format/tone or ask it to draw inspiration from them.
When to use: If you need consistent formatting or creative ideation on information not in the training data.

Chain-of-Thought

Ask the AI to explain its thought process step-by-step, or follow a series of detailed instructions doing so, instead of jumping straight to the answer (seen above a few times).
When to use: When you need accurate reasoning for problem solving, if you keep getting surface-level outputs, or to mimic the “reasoning” of advanced models.

Prompt Chaining

Breaking one big task into smaller conversations, each built context/prompts that optimize that specific step so the output of one conversation builds on the previous.
When to use: You’re trying to uplevel to the “AI is a Chain of Super-Powered Personas”, you’re trying to test automated workflows, or creatively explore lines of thought/production efficiently using AI.

Extra Techniques I Personally Learned

Iterative Prompting

Prompt engineering requires trial and error, web searches, and creative exploration. This skill takes weeks to months to develop, so keep practicing and be patient with yourself

Just enough Context

The goal of prompting is to provide the right context for the model to solve your problem, not to dump as many words as possible into the model to make it “learn”. For smaller tasks, this may mean you limit the prompt to 20 words while other tasks require robust agents with persistent context.

Use Weird Formatting/Characters

LLM training data is mostly plain text. Special characters (!@#$%^&*) and ALL CAPS are rare, giving them stronger semantic weight which makes it helpful when emphasizing critical requirements.
For example: **[MY PRIMARY GOALS OFTEN LOOK LIKE THIS]**

Limitations Beyond this Article


RAG and “reasoning” in advanced models have mitigated typical LLM limitations like factual accuracy and critical thinking. The biggest remaining drawbacks are:

Context or Working Memory

LLMs fixed context windows cause reasoning errors, hallucinations, and inability to retrieve all relevant data. Humans have superior working memory that improves by chunking tasks or or various learning techniques.

  • Solution: Improve your prompting to match the model’s context. Provide examples. Use “Thinking” or “Reasoning” mode for self-reflection.

Learning

LLMs store some conversations and update through retraining, but don’t rebuild their architecture with each question like babies learning, or develop long-term memory like humans remembering preferences

  • Solution: Create persistent contexts (fancy term is “Agent”) with relevant information and prompts (with api protocols like real “agentic AI”) that don’t overload its context.

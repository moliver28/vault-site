The Wrong Question About AI and Your UX Skills

The Wrong Question About AI and Your UX Skills

Properties1
tagsai-trust--and--adoption, human-ai-collaboration, career-development, frameworks--and--models, industry-analysis

Nov 15, 202617 min read

**Status:** First Draft **Estimated read time:** \~12 minutes **Working thesis:** The right question is not how to keep your skills sharp under AI. It is which skills will pay rent in 2030, and which ones you are still rehearsing out of habit. ## A Confession Before the Argument Last month I caught myself opening a blank document and typing out a discussion guide from scratch. I had an LLM open in another tab. I knew, in the analytical part of my brain, that the LLM would have produced a serviceable first draft in fifteen seconds, and that I would then have spent my real cognitive effort on what mattered: whether the questions actually mapped to the decision the team had committed to making, whether the order created demand effects, whether the constructs were operationalized the same way they would be coded. I did not do that. I wrote the draft myself. Slowly. By hand. Why? Because writing a discussion guide is something I am good at. It is one of the things that makes me feel like a researcher. There is a particular pleasure in choosing the right opening question, and I did not want to give that pleasure up. I am telling you this because I think a lot of us are doing some version of this, and I do not want the rest of this essay to read like a scolding. The instinct to keep your hands on your craft is not naive. It is not Luddism. It is, in many ways, the most professionally serious instinct we have. But I have come to believe it is pointed at the wrong target, and the cost of leaving it pointed there is bigger than we have admitted. This is not an essay about how to use AI better. It is an essay about which question to ask in the first place. ## What Most of the Conversation Gets Wrong The dominant question in our field right now is some variation of: *how do I make sure I do not lose any skills?* Practice your synthesis muscles. Hand-code a transcript every so often. Write the draft yourself first. Treat the AI as a junior partner, not a co-author. The advice is everywhere, and most of it is well-intentioned. It is also, I think, a question that cannot be answered. The skill list of a working UX researcher is enormous. You have screening logic, sampling math, study design, instrument writing, moderation craft, transcription, coding, synthesis, theming, visualization, narrative construction, executive translation, stakeholder negotiation, and the social engineering of getting any of it acted on. Nobody has the practice budget to keep all of it sharp without AI help. Trying to is a recipe for either burnout or quiet decline. The researchers I know who are most committed to *not losing their skills* are also the most exhausted, and not coincidentally. There is a second problem. The probability that an LLM will be available to help write your next interview script, screener, or readout is one hundred percent. Not eighty. Not *depends on policy*. One hundred. Any skill you are practicing on the assumption that the AI will not be there is a skill you are practicing for a world that no longer exists. That is not a moral failing. It is just a fact about the timeline we live on. So we are defending a position that cannot be held, with a budget we do not have, against a tool that is not going away. This is what makes the question feel impossible. It *is* impossible. We are asking it wrong. ## What the Worry Gets Right Before I propose a different question, I want to take the existing one seriously, because there is real wisdom inside it. The cognitive science backs up the worry. A 2025 Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon survey of 319 knowledge workers using GenAI tools at least once a week found that the more confident people were in the AI, the less critical thinking they did with its output. Higher self-confidence led to more scrutiny; higher AI-confidence led to less. The authors describe a shift in what critical thinking even *means* in AI-assisted work, away from production and toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship \[1\]. A randomized lab study published in the *British Journal of Educational Technology* found that ChatGPT made student essays better while making the students worse, a phenomenon the authors call *metacognitive laziness* \[2\]. Ganuthula's theoretical model formalizes this as the Paradox of Augmentation: the capacities AI augments are precisely the ones that atrophy under that augmentation \[3\]. Aviation has known this for thirty years. NASA work on cockpit automation found that pilots who lean heavily on autopilot lose specific cognitive skills, particularly the ability to keep track of the aircraft's situation without instrument support \[4\]. Medical imaging research warns that AI assistance can both accelerate skill decay in experts and hinder skill development in novices, while making both groups *less* aware of the deficit \[5\]. So the worry is not paranoid. The risk is documented. The instinct to keep your own hands on the work, to maintain manual skill so you can catch the system when it is quietly wrong, is the same instinct that keeps pilots alive and patients diagnosed. It is the most professionally responsible instinct in the field. I do not want to argue against that instinct. I want to argue that we are pointing it at the wrong layer of the work. ## The Layer Confusion Here is the move. For about thirty years, in our craft, you could not produce the artifact without the cognition. You could not write a good discussion guide unless you understood demand effects, construct operationalization, and decision-relevance. You could not synthesize a transcript unless you could hold competing interpretations in your head and adjudicate between them. The artifact was downstream evidence of the thinking. We treated production as the skill because, for our entire careers, production *required* the skill. The two were welded together. LLMs decoupled them. You can now produce a plausible-looking discussion guide in fifteen seconds with no real understanding of what makes it good. A junior researcher cannot easily tell the difference. Sometimes a senior researcher cannot either, especially when the AI has gotten the surface right and the substance wrong in a subtle way. This is exactly the medical imaging case from the literature: the output looks competent enough that the user's metacognitive alarm does not fire, and they accept work that they would have caught had they done it themselves \[5\]. Notice what this means. The skill we were protecting, *writing a guide from scratch*, is not actually the skill that kept the research honest. The skill that kept the research honest was the *evaluation* happening in the senior researcher's head when they reviewed the junior's draft: does the opening establish behavioral, not attitudinal, framing? Do the warmups validate recruitment, or burn time? Does the question order create demand effects? Are constructs operationalized the same way they will be coded? Does each question map to a decision someone has actually committed to making? Nothing on that list is something an LLM does well unsupervised. All of it is what experienced researchers were *already* doing implicitly, every time they reviewed a draft. The skill was always there. We just got to keep it hidden inside the production work, where we did not have to name it. The AI did not eliminate the skill. It promoted it. It moved evaluation from background quality control to foreground job description. ## The Reframe So the question changes, and the change is a kindness. *How do I keep all my skills sharp?* is unwinnable. *Which skills will pay rent in 2030, and which can I let go of with a clear conscience?* is a question I can actually answer. It gives me permission to stop defending positions that are not worth defending, and to put my scarce cognitive effort where it compounds. I want to be honest about what this asks. Letting go of skills you spent years building is a kind of grief. The pleasure I take in writing a good discussion guide is not negligible, and I am not going to pretend it is. The migration from production to evaluation is harder, less visible, and harder to take pride in. There is no equivalent of the satisfaction you get from a beautifully constructed instrument. The new work is mostly judgment exercised privately, and it leaves fewer artifacts to point at. That is a real cost. I do not think the field has reckoned with it. Most of the AI-and-UX content out there talks about productivity gains and tool stacks. Almost none of it talks about what it feels like to lose the visible craft and have to rebuild your professional identity around something quieter. We should talk about it. We should let people grieve. But the reframe is still the right one. Because the alternative, defending the production layer, is what gets us treated as a cost center. It is what makes researchers replaceable in the eyes of an organization that has noticed the AI can produce comparable artifacts at zero marginal cost. The path through this is not louder defense of the artifact. It is honest migration to the layer where our judgment actually lives. ## What Is Worth Releasing, What Is Worth Honoring A simple way to think about this is two columns. Skills you can release with a clear conscience because they have stopped distinguishing you, and skills worth honoring and deepening because they are now the entire job.
**Skills to Release with Grace****Skills to Honor and Deepen**
Producing the discussion guide from scratchEvaluating the guide for construct validity and decision-relevance
Writing the screenerSpecifying the population frame and quota math the screener has to satisfy
First-pass coding and theme productionAuditing AI-generated codes for construct drift, salience bias, and missing voices
Drafting the readout deckFraming what decision the readout has to unblock and what to leave out
Recall of every method's procedureMatch-making between business question, evidence standard, and method stack
Producing personas and journey mapsGoverning what gets canonized as a persona and what stays a working hypothesis
Note-taking during sessionsLive sense-making, including reading what was *not* said
Knowing the literatureTranslating the literature into operational decisions stakeholders can act on
The pattern is consistent. Production gets cheaper. Judgment gets scarcer. The skill we cared about all along was always in the right column. We just got to leave it implicit, because the left column required it as a prerequisite. That is no longer true. The cognition has to stand on its own now, and that is harder, but it is also more honest. The left column is not where the craft lived. It is where the craft *showed*. The right column is where the craft actually was, all along. ## A Closer Look at the Discussion Guide Let me drill into one row, because the abstract framing only does so much work. When a senior researcher reviewed a junior's discussion guide draft, what were they actually doing? Not admiring the prose. They were running a private checklist that almost never got written down: - Does the opening establish *behavioral* framing? "Tell me about the last time you..." rather than "How do you usually feel about..."? - Are the warmup questions doing recruitment validation, confirming the person is in the population we sampled for, or are they wasted minutes? - Does the question order create demand effects on later answers? Does asking about brand affinity early bias responses to feature questions later? - Are constructs operationalized in the instrument the same way they will be operationalized in the analysis? If we are measuring *trust*, is the question worded the same way the codebook will define it? - Does each question map to a decision the team has committed to making, or are some of them there because they are interesting but unactionable? - Are we leaving room for the participant to surprise us, or has the question structure already pre-determined the answer space? That list is the actual skill. It always was. The LLM did not take it. It just stopped letting us hide it inside the writing work. Senior researchers who are good at this can run that checklist on an AI-drafted guide in two minutes and produce a better guide than they could have written from scratch in an hour. That is not a loss of craft. That is craft finally being able to operate at the speed of the questions it cares about. Junior researchers cannot run that checklist yet. That is the real concern under all of this, and it is one I take seriously. The implicit apprenticeship of writing your own drafts and getting them critiqued was how most of us learned to evaluate. If the AI writes the drafts, where does that learning happen? I do not have a complete answer. What I have is a guess: the apprenticeship has to become explicit. Senior researchers will need to teach the evaluation checklist directly, rather than letting juniors absorb it through years of red ink. That is harder to do, and the field is behind on it. But it is the actual problem, and it is not solved by junior researchers writing more drafts by hand. ## The Strategic Stakes Step back from the individual practitioner for a moment. The field-level argument is this: UX research has been stuck between two equally weak positions on AI. *Panic* (the AI will replace us) and *denial* (the AI cannot do real research). Both positions leave UXR positioned as a *production function*, the team that makes the artifacts. That position is exactly the one labor markets punish hardest under automation. Stanford HAI's work on automation finds that occupations that lose inexpert tasks while gaining expert tasks see employment fall and wages rise. Occupations that gain inexpert tasks while losing expert ones see the opposite \[6\]. The IMF's 2026 analysis finds that AI-related skills carry a wage premium, but that AI-vulnerable occupations are seeing entry-level employment drop where AI adoption is high \[7\]. HBS research by Bojinov and McFowland finds that GenAI lets novices reach further into adjacent tasks, but cannot turn novices into experts; the expert layer is where the durable human value lives \[8\]. The pattern is clear. Production is becoming a commodity. Expert evaluation is where the wage premium and the strategic influence both sit. Researchers who migrate up the stack get treated as strategic infrastructure. Researchers who keep defending the production layer get treated as overhead the AI is gradually making redundant. This is not a tooling argument. It is a positioning argument, and it is the argument the field needs to be having instead of the one it is currently having. ## What This Looks Like in Practice If the reframe is right, here is what changes about how a researcher spends their week. **Less time on first drafts. More time specifying.** Specifying the decision a study has to unblock, before any instrument is written. Specifying the construct definitions that the instrument and the analysis both have to share. Specifying the evidence standard that will count as *we know enough.* **Less time producing artifacts. More time auditing them.** Treating an AI-drafted guide, screener, codebook, or readout as a defendant, not a deliverable. What is missing? What construct has drifted? Whose voice is absent? What question is the artifact answering that nobody actually asked? **Less time on methods recall. More time on methods match-making.** The skill is no longer remembering what a critical incident technique looks like in procedure. The LLM has that. The skill is knowing when CIT is the right tool for the question on the table, and when a diary study, a switch interview, or a think-aloud would buy more decision-relevant evidence. **Less time finishing decks. More time framing what the deck has to do.** What decision is on the table? What is the executive afraid of? What finding, if true, would change the roadmap, and what finding would not? What are we choosing *not* to include, and why? **Less individual production. More apprenticeship.** The evaluation skills that used to transfer implicitly have to transfer explicitly now. That is a leadership task, and it is on the senior tier of the profession to figure it out. None of this is glamorous. None of it produces the satisfying artifact-shaped output that used to be the felt reward of the work. But all of it is the work that actually moves a research practice from cost center to revenue driver, and it is all work the AI cannot do unsupervised. ## A Closing Invitation If you are tired of trying to defend everything, you have permission to stop. Pick the three skills that govern your judgment. Mine, this year, are construct operationalization, decision-framing for stakeholders, and live sense-making in unmoderated sessions. They are not the skills I am best at. They are the skills I most want to compound. I am going to put my practice budget there. I am going to let the AI take a much larger share of the production work, and I am going to spend the time I save on getting better at evaluation. I expect this to be uncomfortable for a while. I expect to grieve the visible craft, the way you grieve any identity you outgrow. If you do this, you are not abandoning the craft. You are finally being asked to do it without the artifact as cover. That is harder. It is also more honest. The craft was always in the judgment, and the judgment is what is left. The right question was never *how do I keep my skills.* It was always *which skills are worth keeping.* The answer to that question, today, is shorter than it used to be, and that is not a loss. That is the work finally showing itself. --- ## References \[1\] Lee, H., Sarkar, A., Tankelevitch, L., Drosos, I., Rintel, S., Banks, R., & Wilson, N. (2025).

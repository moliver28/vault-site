The Most Accurate Mental Model of an LLM

The Most Accurate Mental Model of an LLM

Properties1
tagshuman-ai-collaboration, frameworks--and--models

Nov 14, 20265 min read

Think of an LLM as a brain without a body, consciousness, or goals. It has structures that look like cognition, but they work fundamentally differently. Understanding these differences unlocks better prompting and realistic expectations.
This mental model synthesizes foundational research on Transformers, attention mechanisms, chain-of-thought prompting, and retrieval-augmented generation into one brain-inspired framework.

The Core Insight

An LLM predicts the next word. That’s it.
To do this well, it needs to:

  1. Understand what each word means
  2. Remember what came before
  3. Figure out which previous words matter most
  4. Use patterns learned from reading billions of sentences
    Every capability you’ve seen—writing code, analyzing data, creative writing—emerges from this single mechanism: sophisticated next-word prediction.

How It Actually Works (Technical, But Accessible)

Step 1: Tokenization

Text gets chopped into small pieces called “tokens.” Think Lego blocks—not individual letters or whole words, but common chunks:

  • “unhappiness” → [“un”, “happi”, “ness”]
  • “The cat sat” → [“The”, ” cat”, ” sat”]
    A typical LLM knows about 50,000 different tokens.

Step 2: Embeddings

Each token becomes a list of numbers called a “vector”—a point in space with hundreds of dimensions. Similar words end up near each other. “King” and “queen” are close together. “King” and “pizza” are far apart.

Step 3: Attention

For each word, the model asks three questions:

  • Query: “What information do I need?”
  • Key: “What does each other word offer?”
  • Value: “What’s the actual content?”
    It calculates scores showing how much each word should “attend to” each other word, then creates a new representation mixing the most relevant information together.

Step 4: Prediction

After many layers of attention and processing, the model outputs a probability for every possible next token:

  • “mat” → 35% likely
  • “floor” → 25% likely
  • “couch” → 15% likely
  • (and 49,997 other options with smaller percentages)
    Then it samples from this distribution to pick the actual next word.

Step 5: Repeat

Add that word to the sequence, predict the next one. Keep going until you hit a stopping point.

What This Explains About LLM Behavior

Why Chain-of-Thought Prompting Works

Asking the model to “think step by step” forces it to use its context window more effectively, building up intermediate results.
Instead of:

Q: What is 15% of 80?
A: 12
You get:
Q: What is 15% of 80?
A: Let me work through this step-by-step:

  1. Convert 15% to decimal: 0.15
  2. Multiply: 0.15 × 80 = 12
    Therefore, the answer is 12.
    Showing reasoning steps improves accuracy on complex problems by 10-30%.

Why Hallucinations Happen

The model doesn’t “know” things—it predicts what words should come next based on patterns. If a plausible-sounding but false statement fits the pattern, it gets generated with confidence.

Why Temperature Matters

Low temperature (0.2): Nearly deterministic. “The capital of France is Paris” (99% confident). Best for factual tasks.
High temperature (1.5): Very random. “The capital of France is… Paris, Lyon, Marseille, or perhaps the Eiffel Tower’s shadow.” Best for creative writing.

Fundamental Strengths

  1. Parallel processing: Processes entire sequences simultaneously via attention
  2. Long-range dependencies: Can connect information 1000+ tokens apart
  3. Transfer learning: Knowledge from general training helps specialized tasks
  4. Flexibility: Same architecture works for text, code, images (with tokenization)
  5. Contextual understanding: Captures nuanced semantic relationships

Fundamental Weaknesses

  1. No true reasoning: Only pattern matching. Struggles with novel logical problems.
  2. Hallucination: Confidently generates plausible but false information.
  3. Fixed knowledge: Cannot learn from conversation. Each response is independent.
  4. Context limits: Cannot process books or long documents in one pass.
  5. Bias: Inherits biases from training data.
  6. No world model: Doesn’t understand physics, causation, or object permanence.

The Mental Model Visualization

Key Takeaways

  1. LLMs are pattern-matching engines, not thinking machines. They excel at statistical completion but lack genuine understanding.
  2. You are the executive controller. You’re not conversing with an intelligent being—you’re programming a sophisticated autocomplete system through natural language.
  3. Attention is the breakthrough. It lets models focus on relevant context dynamically.
  4. Knowledge is frozen at training time. Stored in billions of weights, not in a searchable database.
  5. Generation is probabilistic. Same input can give different outputs. Temperature controls randomness.
  6. Limitations are fundamental. These aren’t bugs to be fixed but inherent to the architecture. No amount of scaling fixes lack of reasoning.

Best mental model: Think of it as an incredibly sophisticated “autocomplete” that learned patterns from reading the entire internet, with you as the programmer using English instead of code.
This explains both what LLMs can do amazingly well (pattern completion, style mimicry, contextual generation) and what they fundamentally cannot do (genuine reasoning, learning from experience, maintaining coherent beliefs across conversations).

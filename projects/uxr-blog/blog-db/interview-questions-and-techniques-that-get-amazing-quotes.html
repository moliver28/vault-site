Interview Questions and Techniques That Get Amazing Quotes

Interview Questions and Techniques That Get Amazing Quotes

Properties1
tagsqualitative-methods

Nov 14, 202615 min read

Most interview transcripts are full of data and completely empty of anything quotable. You get “It’s fine” and “It works for what I need” and thirty variations of polite nothing.
Then, once in a while, a participant says something so specific that it stops a room. A single quote pulls a feature off the backlog and into the next sprint. A single follow-up question redirects a quarter’s worth of product decisions.
The difference between those two outcomes is almost never the participant. It’s the question.
Most UX researchers know how to conduct interviews. But few have a systematic approach to extracting the kind of quotes that make stakeholders lean forward in their chairs. After years of running interviews, building script libraries, and studying techniques from Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) to the Critical Incident Technique (CIT), I’ve found that quote-worthy answers come from six specific types of questions.
This article breaks down each type, gives you the exact questions to use, and explains why each one works. By the end, you’ll have a toolkit for turning any interview into a source of quotes that build empathy and drive decisions.

Why Most Interview Questions Produce Forgettable Answers

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: generic questions produce generic answers.
When you ask “How do you feel about this product?” you get “It’s fine.” When you ask “What do you think of the navigation?” you get “It seems okay, I guess.” These answers are technically data, but they’re useless in a stakeholder presentation. They don’t move anyone.
Here’s what the difference looks like in practice:

Generic question: “How do you feel about the reporting feature?”
What you get: “It’s okay. It works for what I need.”


Technique question: “Walk me through the last time you had to pull a report right before a deadline. What was happening?”
What you get: “It was 9pm on a Sunday. The board wanted the risk report by Monday morning, and I’d already spent three hours trying to get the filters to work. I ended up exporting everything to Excel and doing it manually. I almost cried.”

Same participant. Same topic. Completely different output. The second answer has a story, an emotion, a workaround, and a business cost. That’s a quote you can put in front of a VP and watch the room go silent.
The problem isn’t the participant. It’s the question. Most interview scripts are built around topics the researcher wants to cover, not around the types of responses that actually influence decisions.
The NNGroup funnel technique gives us the structural foundation: start broad and open-ended, then narrow to specifics. But the funnel is about structure. What goes inside the funnel matters more.

What makes a quote “stakeholder-ready”? It contains at least one of these: a specific moment, a real emotion, a concrete consequence, or the user’s natural language for a problem. The techniques below are designed to hit these targets reliably.

The 6 Types of Questions That Produce Powerful Quotes

Think of these as six lenses you can apply to any research topic. Each one targets a different dimension of the user’s experience and produces a different kind of quote. You don’t need all six in every interview. Pick the ones that match your research goals.

1. The Emotional Confession

What it produces: Raw, honest statements about how users feel. These are the quotes that build empathy and remind stakeholders that real humans use their product.
Why it works: Emotions are memorable. When a VP of Product reads “I felt stupid because I couldn’t figure out how to do something that should be simple,” that sticks in a way that a usability score never will. Emotional quotes bypass the analytical brain and hit the part that drives action.
The techniques behind it:
The Critical Incident Technique (Flanagan, 1954) is the gold standard here. CIT asks participants to recall specific, significant moments, and then explicitly probes the emotional dimension of those moments. Retrospective probing captures emotions immediately after task completion while they’re still fresh. Laddering interviews trace surface reactions down to core values.

Questions to use:

“How did you feel during this incident?"
"What emotions were you experiencing at different points?"
"How did your emotions change as the situation unfolded?"
"How does that make you feel?” (during think-aloud)
“What was your emotional state immediately afterward?"
"Did your feelings match your expectations, or were you surprised by your emotional reaction?"
"What emotions do you associate with the old solution vs. the new one?“

**Pro Tip:** Don't ask about emotions in the abstract. **Anchor them to a specific moment first.** "How did you feel when you realized the report hadn't saved?" will always produce a richer answer than "How do you feel about the save feature?" The CIT approach of first establishing the incident, then probing the emotional response, is what makes this work.

2. The Vivid Story

What it produces: Detailed, narrative accounts of specific experiences. These are the quotes that paint a picture so clear that stakeholders can see the user’s world.
Why it works: Stories are how humans make sense of experience. When a participant tells you “I was sitting at my desk at 11pm trying to get this report out before the board meeting, and I clicked export and nothing happened,” that narrative context transforms a bug report into a human moment. Research on narrative interviews consistently shows that story-based questions produce richer, more detailed responses than direct questions.
The techniques behind it:
The Switch Interview (from JTBD methodology) reconstructs the complete timeline of a decision. CIT captures the full situational context of critical moments. Narrative interviews let participants structure their own story with minimal interruption.

Questions to use:

“Can you walk me through that specific moment or day?"
"What was going on in your life at that time?"
"What was the specific situation or context when this incident occurred?"
"Tell me about the last time you needed to [achieve outcome]. Walk me through what was happening right before you started."
"What happened next after that first thought?"
"Walk me through the entire journey from start to finish in chronological order."
"Paint me a picture of what that moment looked like.”

**Pro Tip:** The magic phrase is **"walk me through."** It signals to the participant that you want a blow-by-blow account, not a summary. When they start summarizing, gently redirect with *"Can you slow down and tell me exactly what happened at that point?"* The NNGroup interview guide approach recommends these [critical-incident style "example questions"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interview-guide/) because they prompt specific recall rather than general impressions.

3. The Pain Confession

What it produces: Statements about frustration, struggle, and unmet needs. These are the quotes that justify investment because they quantify the cost of inaction.
Why it works: Stakeholders approve budgets to solve problems. When a user says “I spend two hours every week manually copying data between systems because there’s no integration,” that’s not just a pain point. That’s a business case. The Circumstance-Driven JTBD approach focuses on the user’s current state and frustrations, deliberately avoiding product-specific questions so participants describe their struggles in their own terms.
The techniques behind it:
Circumstance-Driven JTBD maps current frustrations and workarounds. The Switch Interview’s push forces reveal what drives users away from their current solution. CIT’s impact and consequence probes capture what happens when things go wrong.

Questions to use:

“What’s not working about your current approach?"
"What takes too much time in your current process?"
"What workarounds have you created?"
"What frustrated you most about your previous solution?"
"What’s the most annoying part of this process?"
"What would have happened if you had done nothing?"
"What keeps breaking down in your current approach?"
"What do you have to think about or remember that you wish you didn’t?”

**Pro Tip:** The question *"What workarounds have you created?"* is one of the most underrated questions in UX research. **Workarounds are proof of pain.** They show that the problem is bad enough that users invested time building their own solution. When you present a workaround to stakeholders, you're showing them evidence that the problem is real and that users are *already trying to solve it themselves.*

**See It In Action: One Question Chain, Four Quote Types** Imagine you're interviewing a procurement manager about their compliance workflow. Watch how a single thread produces multiple quote types in a natural conversation: **You:** *"Walk me through the last time you had to prepare a compliance report."* (Vivid Story) **Them:** *"Last quarter, right before the audit..."* and they tell you the full story. **You:** *"What was the hardest part of that?"* (Pain Confession) **Them:** *"I spent two full days manually cross-referencing three different spreadsheets because nothing talks to each other."* **You:** *"How did that make you feel?"* (Emotional Confession) **Them:** *"Honestly? I felt like I was failing at my job. I'm supposed to be managing risk, not copy-pasting cells."* **You:** *"Tell me more about that."* (Depth Probe) **Them:** *"It's like... I went to school for this, I have 15 years of experience, and I'm doing data entry. That's not what I signed up for."* Four questions. Four quote types. One continuous, natural conversation. **The techniques aren't separate tools you switch between. They're lenses you layer onto a single thread.** --- ## 4. The Decision Reveal **What it produces:** Statements about why users chose, switched, or abandoned a product or workflow. These quotes inform product strategy because they expose the real criteria users care about. **Why it works:** Understanding *why* someone switched to your product (or a competitor's) is worth more than any feature request list. The Switch Interview methodology, grounded in JTBD theory, reconstructs the four forces of progress: push (away from old), pull (toward new), anxiety (about switching), and habit (keeping the status quo). Laddering interviews trace surface preferences down to core values, revealing the "why behind the why." **The techniques behind it:** The **Switch Interview** maps the full decision journey. **Laddering** connects features to consequences to values. **Circumstance-Driven JTBD** reveals decision criteria without anchoring to existing solutions. ### Questions to use: > *"What specifically triggered you to think 'I need to do something about this'?"* > *"What made that moment different from all the times before when you thought about it but didn't act?"* > *"What attracted you to this new solution? What made you think 'this is it'?"* > *"What were you worried about with this new solution?"* > *"What almost made you not do it?"* > *"Why is that important to you?"* (then repeat with each answer, laddering deeper) > *"How are you deciding what approach to take? What makes one approach better than another for your situation?"* **Pro Tip:** The **Switch Interview's "first thought" question** is incredibly powerful: *"When did you first start thinking about looking for a solution like this?"* Most researchers jump straight to evaluation criteria. But reconstructing the *timeline* from first thought to final decision reveals the struggling moment, the trigger event, and the emotional context that generic preference questions completely miss. --- ## 5. The "In Their Own Words" Moment **What it produces:** Natural language, mental models, and metaphors that reveal how users actually think about a problem. These quotes resonate because they sound *real*, not filtered through research jargon. **Why it works:** When a user describes your product as *"it's like trying to drive a car where the steering wheel is in the backseat,"* that metaphor communicates the problem more effectively than any heuristic evaluation. The Think-Aloud Protocol captures real-time cognitive processes and natural vocabulary. Projective techniques unlock language that participants can't access through direct questioning. **The techniques behind it:** The **Think-Aloud Protocol** captures unfiltered narration during task performance. **Projective Techniques** (sentence completion, metaphors, personification) access implicit beliefs that direct questions miss. The **Switch Interview's language and framing** section captures how users naturally describe their needs. ### Questions to use: > *"Tell me what's going through your mind."* (during task) > *"What are you looking for right now?"* > *"How would you explain to a friend what you needed?"* > *"What words would you use to describe your problem?"* > *"Complete this sentence: 'Using this product is like __ because __.'"* > *"If this product were a person, how would you describe their personality?"* > *"How would you describe this in your own words?"* **Pro Tip:** **Write down exact words.** When a participant says *"I felt lost"* during a think-aloud, don't paraphrase it as *"user experienced navigation confusion"* in your notes. The participant's actual vocabulary is your most persuasive asset. In your research report, use their language, not yours. Stakeholders connect with *"I felt lost"* in a way they never will with researcher-speak. --- ## 6. The Depth Probe **What it produces:** Richer, more specific, more quotable versions of answers the participant already gave. Depth probes don't generate quotes on their own. They transform surface-level answers into ones worth presenting. **Why it works:** Participants often give the "headline" version of their experience first. The details, the emotions, and the specifics that make a quote compelling are just one follow-up question away. The [NNGroup funnel technique](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-funnel-technique-in-qualitative-user-research/) formalizes this: start broad, then use probing questions to dig into areas of interest. CIT includes entire sections dedicated to specificity probes, causal probes, and impact probes. Laddering's repeated "why" questions peel back layers until you reach core values. **The techniques behind it:** **CIT's open-ended and specificity probes** push for concrete details. The **Funnel Technique** structures the flow from broad to narrow. **Laddering's "why" chains** go deeper with each iteration. ### Questions to use: > *"Tell me more about that."* > *"What do you mean by \[specific term they used\]?"* > *"Can you give me a concrete example of what you're describing?"* > *"Why is that important to you?"* (repeat) > *"What specifically made you think that?"* > *"Help me understand what you experienced in that moment."* > *"What's the most significant aspect of this to you?"* > *"What surprised you most about this experience?"* **Pro Tip:** The single most powerful probe in all of qualitative research is **silence.** After a participant answers, wait 3 to 5 seconds before responding. The discomfort of silence often prompts them to fill it with the deeper, more honest version of what they just said. Combine silence with a simple *"Tell me more about that"* and you'll be amazed at what surfaces. --- ## Putting It Together: Build Your Quote-Generating Script You don't need to memorize all of these. Instead, **build a modular interview script** that matches your research goals. Here's how: ### Step 1: Pick your research goal
**If you need to...****Lead with...**
Build empathy for user painEmotional Confessions + Pain Confessions
Justify a product investmentPain Confessions + Decision Reveals
Understand why users switchDecision Reveals + Vivid Stories
Inform content strategy or terminology"In Their Own Words" + Depth Probes
Evaluate a prototype or conceptVivid Stories + Emotional Confessions
### Step 2: Select 3 to 5 core questions from the relevant categories Don't overload your script. A 60-minute interview has room for about 5 to 8 core questions, each with follow-up probes. Pick questions from 2 to 3 of the six categories above. ### Step 3: Always include depth probes Regardless of which categories you choose, **keep depth probes in your back pocket for every single question.** Questions like *"Tell me more"* and *"Can you give me an example?"* should be reflexive. They're the difference between a surface answer and a quotable one. ### Step 4: Use the funnel structure Start each section with your broadest question. Let the participant talk. Then use progressively specific probes to dig into the moments, emotions, and details that produce great quotes. --- ## Quick Reference: The 10 Most Versatile Quote-Generating Questions If you want a starting point, these 10 questions work across almost any research context. Keep them as a cheat sheet: 1. *"Walk me through the last time you \[did the activity\]. Start from the very beginning."* 2. *"What was going on in your life at that time?"* 3. *"What specifically triggered you to think 'I need to do something about this'?"* 4. *"What's not working about your current approach?"* 5. *"What workarounds have you created?"* 6. *"How did you feel when that happened?"* 7. *"What made that moment different from all the times before?"* 8. *"What would have happened if you had done nothing?"* 9. *"Why is that important to you?"* 10. *"Tell me more about that."* **Start here.** If you're new to these techniques, pick 3 to 4 questions from this list and add them to your next interview script. Practice using the depth probes after each answer. You'll notice the difference in your transcripts immediately. --- ## What Happens Next Great quotes don't help anyone if they stay buried in a transcript. Once you've captured them: - **Tag quotes by type** (emotional, story, pain, decision, natural language) during analysis - **Create a highlight reel** of your 5 to 10 strongest quotes for stakeholder presentations - **Pair each quote with a design implication** so the insight leads directly to action - **Use exact user language** in your personas, journey maps, and requirement documents The techniques in this article come from decades of research methodology, from Flanagan's Critical Incident Technique (1954) to modern JTBD switch interviews and the NNGroup's funnel technique. They work because they're grounded in how people actually recall and describe their experiences. The best part? These aren't skills you need special training for. They're questions. **You can start using them in your very next interview.** Here's your challenge: before your next interview, pick **one question from each of three categories** above and add them to your script. After the interview, review your transcript and highlight every quote that could stand on its own in a stakeholder presentation. Compare that count to your last interview. I'll bet the number doubles. These techniques are compound interest for your research craft. Each interview gets better. Each transcript gets richer. And over time, you build a library of quotes that doesn't just report what users said. It moves entire teams to action.

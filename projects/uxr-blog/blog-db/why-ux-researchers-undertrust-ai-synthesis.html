Why UX Researchers Undertrust AI Synthesis

The question of why UX researchers distrust AI synthesis has a sharper answer than “AI makes mistakes.” It is a specific, calibrated response to the moment where extraction ends and interpretation begins.
Most researchers are comfortable using AI to organize a mess. They trust the tool to cluster a hundred quotes into five buckets or to summarize a transcript. But the moment the tool is asked to weigh those buckets and recommend a product direction, trust thins.
Consider a lead researcher under the pressure of a two-week agile sprint. She runs her interviews through a synthesis tool that surfaces three plausible themes. One of those themes is a headline she needs for a stakeholder deck. When she tries to trace that theme back to the original evidence, she finds the tool cannot show her the “why.” There is no path from the raw quote to the final claim.
She cannot defend the finding, so she withholds it and reverts to manual coding. She loses the sprint, but she saves her credibility.
The distrust is a rational reaction to a lack of epistemic access, not a bug in the researcher’s mindset.

Can AI do qualitative coding? Researchers already say yes

The industry is absorbing AI, not avoiding it. In Lyssna’s 2026 trends survey of 100 UX researchers, 88 percent named AI-assisted analysis and synthesis the top trend [1]. This is not fringe behavior. A User Interviews survey of 1,093 researchers found that 77.1 percent use AI in some part of their work, with qualitative coding standing as the most popular analysis use case [2].
The adoption is heavy, but the anxiety is higher. Researchers are using these tools as first-pass filters to handle the scale of modern data, yet they remain deeply skeptical of the output. They have accepted AI as a librarian, but they have not yet accepted it as an analyst.

Why do UX researchers distrust AI synthesis anyway?

The gap between adoption and trust is best seen as a gradient. Lyssna found that researchers trust AI far more for identifying patterns (61.0 percent) than for translating those patterns into recommendations (47.6 percent) [1].
This spread tracks the interpretive depth of the task. Identifying a pattern is largely an act of extraction. It is a search for recurrence. But making a recommendation is an act of judgment. It requires weighing conflicting evidence, understanding the organization’s constraints, and taking a professional risk.
This is where the problem of epistemic access enters. In a human-to-human research partnership, a researcher can ask their colleague, “How did you arrive at this insight?” The colleague can point to specific quotes, explain their reasoning, and admit where the evidence is thin.
AI synthesis offers none of this by default. It provides the conclusion without the work. When a tool presents a high-level insight without a visible reasoning path, the researcher does not see a “shortcut.” They see an unaccountable claim. The distrust of AI synthesis is, at its core, a demand for provenance.

Which parts of the distrust are warranted?

It would be a mistake to frame all distrust as a cognitive quirk. Some of it is correct calibration.
Current AI tools often produce hallucinations of meaning. NN/g has noted that synthetic users frequently provide shallow or overly favorable feedback, creating a sycophantic loop that misses real user pain [3].
In more formal evaluations, the misalignment is measurable. An evaluation of AI-assisted deductive coding in MAXQDA found that 47.6 percent of AI-coded segments did not align with manual coding, and the AI’s codes tended to be broader and less fine-grained [4]. Broad codes lose exactly the small cues that signal what a participant actually feels. That last claim is inference; the study reports scope and granularity only.
When an AI misses what participants are actually anxious about, it provides a false signal. Efficiency is beside the point. Researchers who withhold trust in these instances are doing their jobs, not suffering from bias.
The challenge is distinguishing between warranted distrust (the tool is wrong) and structural distrust (the tool is right, but cannot prove it).
Some of the distrust is institutional. In Qualitative Inquiry, 419 experienced qualitative researchers from 32 countries hold that reflexive thematic analysis belongs to the “human research practices requiring a subjective, positioned, and reflexive researcher,” and that generative AI in such approaches is “not methodologically congruent” [5]. In that camp, interpretation is definitionally human work. The researcher’s self is the instrument.

Is this just algorithm aversion?

The industry often labels this skepticism as “algorithm aversion.” This term, coined by Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey [6], describes a specific asymmetry: people lose confidence in an algorithm more quickly than in a human after seeing the same mistake.
In research synthesis, one mistake poisons the whole tool. A single misattributed quote or a glaringly wrong theme in a report does double damage. It is an error, and it is an algorithmic error. In the original experiments, people abandoned the algorithm after watching it err, even when they had seen it outperform the human.
However, applying this decision-science finding to qualitative research requires a caveat. Dietvorst’s original work was established on forecasting tasks with a measurable answer key. Qualitative synthesis is different. There is no “ground truth” to check the AI against. The transfer of the aversion mechanism here is interpretive, not demonstrated.
The deeper driver may be the nature of uncertainty. Dietvorst and Bharti [7] found that as uncertainty rises, people prefer human judgment. They are willing to risk the variance of a human expert for a shot at near-perfection. Qualitative synthesis is a maximally uncertain domain. In the face of irreducible complexity, the researcher’s instinct to hold interpretation close is a survival mechanism.

Why does trust follow the label, not the text?

If the output is what matters, then the tool’s identity should be irrelevant. But the data suggest otherwise.
Research by Akpinar et al. [8] found a striking paradox. Readers with machine-learning expertise could not reliably distinguish between LLM-written and human-written research abstracts. Yet, their trust ratings shifted based on who they believed wrote the text. Fully AI-generated summaries were rated lowest, while human-written text that had been AI-edited was rated highest.
Trust attaches to believed provenance, not measurable quality.
This reveals a critical distinction between provenance transparency and reasoning transparency. Knowing that a human “touched” the report (provenance) provides a comfort blanket, but it does not solve the opacity problem. The researcher is not actually asking, “Did a human do this?” They are asking, “Can I see the reasoning that led to this?”

Why do PMs trust AI research more than researchers do?

This trust gap often creates friction within agile teams. While researchers undertrust the AI, product managers often overestimate it [10].
This is the mirror image of algorithm aversion, known as algorithm appreciation. Logg, Minson, and Moore [9] found that lay people often prefer algorithmic advice, while experts rely more on their own judgment.
This creates a dangerous misalignment. A PM might paste five transcripts into a chatbot, receive a set of confident themes, and present them as findings. These themes are often surface-level, missing the underlying anxiety or nuance a researcher would catch. Because the PM lacks the expert’s “calibration” for what a real insight looks like, they mistake plausibility for accuracy.
The expert withholds trust to protect the quality of the decision. The non-expert accepts the output to increase the speed of the decision. In this gap, the risk of acting on a false signal grows.

How do you decide if an AI synthesis deserves trust?

If trust is a visibility problem, then the fix is a design problem. Trust is recoverable not by making the model smarter, but by making the process visible.
The IBM Research study [10] provides a design specification for this. To move from plausible to usable, AI synthesis tools must provide epistemic access through a trust audit. When evaluating a tool, look for these five requirements:

  1. Bidirectional Traceability. Can every theme be traced back to specific quotes, codes, and the original transcript context? And can you go from the quote back to the theme?
  2. Inspectable Rationales. Does the tool explain why a specific segment was included in a cluster?
  3. Default Counter-Evidence. Does the tool actively surface quotes that contradict the emerging theme, or does it only show supporting evidence?
  4. Modifiability. Can the output be edited, challenged, and refined? Dietvorst [11] showed that even a slight ability to modify an algorithm’s output repairs aversion and increases satisfaction.
  5. Human-in-the-Loop Judgment. Does the workflow keep the researcher as the final judge of interpretation, or does it attempt to automate the recommendation?
    A tool buyer who uses this checklist can cut through vendor hype. They stop asking “Is the AI accurate?” and start asking “Is the reasoning transparent?”

The goal is calibrated trust, not more trust

The objective of introducing AI into research is to calibrate trust, not to maximize it.
As Lee and See [12] established, the aim is to match the user’s trust to the system’s actual capability. Over-trust leads to errors of commission (acting on a hallucination); under-trust leads to errors of omission (ignoring a valid pattern).
We must stop treating the “black box” of AI as an insurmountable wall. As Peters [13] argues, human cognition is also epistemically opaque. We do not have full introspective access to how our own brains weigh a thousand disparate quotes to form an insight. The epistemic trust we place in other humans rests on justificatory reasons: they can show us the evidence and explain their method.
Trust in AI synthesis is a specification, not a feeling. When the tool can show its work, the researcher can stop guessing and start auditing.

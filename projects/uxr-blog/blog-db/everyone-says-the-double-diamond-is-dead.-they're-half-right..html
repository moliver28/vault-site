The Double Diamond is Dead. Long Live the Double Diamond

The Double Diamond is Dead. Long Live the Double Diamond

Properties1
tagsframeworks--and--models, industry-analysis

Nov 14, 202616 min read

Everyone says the Double Diamond is dead. They’re half right.

The version that’s dead assumed human-speed discovery. “Discover” meant six weeks of desk research and stakeholder interviews before you had a real picture of the problem space. “Develop” meant a brainstorming session with the same five people who always lead with their confirmation bias. Every phase ran at the speed of one team’s pace reading shared updates, how busy their calendar is, and capacity to synthesize all of the data available to update their product strategy. That was a bandwidth constrain and AI removed those constraint. AI can even fake the “Discover” and “Define” phases reasonably well, which used to require some level of hands-on research.
The structure underneath it is a different matter. The four phases still hold: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. And what each phase protects against is still real. Commit to a problem before you’ve mapped the landscape and you’ll spend months solving the wrong one. Generate solutions before you’ve defined the problem and you’ll build something impressive that nobody needed. Ship without testing and you’ll find out what users actually wanted after you’ve already built the wrong version. The sequence is error-correction. Not ceremony.

Long live the Double Diamond.

AI didn’t kill the framework. It removed the bottleneck on both sides of it.
Expansion phases can now be dramatically wider. You can synthesize across dozens of sources in minutes, see a problem through five expert lenses simultaneously, and surface patterns that would take weeks to find manually. Compression phases can now be more evidence-based. You can rank assumptions, stress-test decisions, and strip solutions to their behavioral core before you commit.
The rhythm is the same. The engine behind it is completely different.
What follows is a framework for using that power deliberately. No technical background required. Just curiosity, a problem worth solving, and the AI tools most people already have on their phones and laptops.
Before we go further: more AI means more decisions. More decisions means more bets on your judgment, and more exposure to being wrong at speed. The only logical response to that is to think better, not just faster.

Faster ≠ Better

Speed without better judgment doesn’t reduce risk. It compounds it.
That’s the thing nobody says when they hand you an AI tool and tell you to move faster. More output per hour is real. But so is more exposure to being wrong. AI is about to automate the execution layer of most product roles: the tactical work of creating, writing, building, and validating that every role is built around. That’s the opportunity. Here’s the catch.
When AI removes friction from execution, you make more decisions per day. A lot more. And every decision is a bet on your judgment. More decisions means more bets. More bets means more exposure to getting it wrong at speed.
Here’s the evidence. Moore’s Law predicted that CPU capability would double every two years. It did. And yet, firms today require 18 times more researchers than they did in the 1970s to sustain the same rate of innovation. Company-level productivity still declines 7-10% annually for nearly 90% of firms. The same pattern shows up in agricultural yields, pharmaceutical development, and materials science.
More compute. More resources. Less output per unit of effort.
The problem was never speed. The problem is how we learn.
The Howard Hughes Medical Institute ran an experiment that proved it.

What Better Looks Like

In 2010, they shifted how they distributed research grants: instead of funding projects, they started funding people.
They extended time horizons. They explicitly tolerated early failures. They built in room for researchers to iterate, learn, and change direction. The bet was that if you gave smart people time and psychological safety, better science would follow.
It did. Publications rose 39%. Top citations rose 96%.
They didn’t move faster. They learned better. And learning better produced more innovation.
The lesson for product teams is uncomfortable but clear. Creating novel ideas that actually provide value is hard. It requires user empathy and shared expertise. It requires the willingness to be wrong early so you can be right when it matters. No amount of AI-accelerated execution changes that.
What AI does change is how you collaborate on the hard part.
Instead of one team synthesizing one problem space at human speed, you can now explore ten problem areas simultaneously. Instead of one brainstorm with the same five people, you can generate wildly different solutions from five distinct expert perspectives in minutes. Instead of weeks of desk research before you have a landscape, you can have a synthesis in an afternoon.
The rate of learning goes up. The quality of decisions goes up with it.
Your customers pay you because their problems are hard. That won’t change. What changes is how much cognitive firepower you bring to those hard problems.
The framework that follows isn’t more work. It’s an easier path to the work that actually matters: understanding what customers need and delivering it without guessing.

What Works (and What Doesn’t)

What Works

Four questions. In this order. Every time.

  1. Discover: “What are all the problems facing the market and our users?”
  2. Define: “Which of those problems has the most impact and is actually ours to solve?”
  3. Develop: “Which solution would reach the most users or the largest market?”
  4. Deliver: “How do we build that solution right now?”
    This is the Double Diamond. Before you write it off as a 2005 relic, consider what it actually guarantees: you cannot commit to a solution before you’ve earned the right to. The human jobs at every phase (asking the right questions, extending empathy, generating options, making judgment calls) don’t disappear. AI makes each one possible at a scale that wasn’t available before.

What Doesn’t Work

Asking LLMs to be truly intelligent doesn’t work. Asking them to reason does.
The distinction matters. When you give an LLM an open-ended problem with no structure around how you want it to think, you tend to get one of four failure modes:

  • Irrelevant overload: more information than the team can use
  • Endless categorization: sophisticated taxonomies that produce no actionable solution
  • Complex solutions to fake problems: solutions that impress in a meeting but won’t earn a single dollar
  • Compounded bias: your assumptions, reflected back at you, louder
    Without guardrails, AI doesn’t help you navigate the decision space. It amplifies chaos inside it.
    The cognitive science behind why this happens is worth a full article on its own. The short version: LLMs are exceptional at recall and comprehension but fall apart at the higher-order thinking (analysis, evaluation, creation) that product decisions actually require. Without structure, they confidently generate the wrong answer. A deeper breakdown of where LLMs succeed and fail across Bloom’s Taxonomy is coming in a future piece.
    The practical point for now: the tool is powerful. An undirected tool is just noise at scale. That structure is the Random Forest Double Diamond.

The Random Forest Double Diamond

Before AI, product development was sequential. You discovered the problem, decided on a solution, designed it, tested it, and shipped it. Each phase waited for the last one to finish. The whole process moved at the speed of one team’s bandwidth.
After AI, work becomes parallel.
Instead of exploring one problem area deeply, you explore ten simultaneously. Instead of generating one solution direction and iterating, you generate wildly different solutions and assess them against each other. Instead of one synthesis pass, you run multiple in minutes and compare the outputs.
Each “dot” in the diagram below represents a decision. Pre-AI, there are relatively few dots per phase. Post-AI, each phase branches into a forest of parallel paths, evaluated through synthesis-level reasoning, compressed back to a decision by the human in the loop.
This is the Random Forest Double Diamond. Same structure. Same rhythm. Completely different density of thinking at every phase.
Navigating it comes down to two questions, held in tension at every phase:

  • Horizontal axis: Why are we doing this phase? (the reasoning that keeps you honest)
  • Vertical axis: How can AI do this better? (the prompting that expands your reach)
    Here’s how those questions look in practice across all four phases.

Discover: Expand the Problem Space

The job of the Discover phase is to fight premature convergence. You want the widest possible view of the problem landscape before you narrow anything down.
The “Why” questions:

  • Why is this problem worth solving for the business?
  • Why do users actually care about this problem?
  • Why do I care? (Honest answer. It shapes your framing.)
    The “How can AI help” questions:
  • What AI role or persona would surface the most useful perspective here?
  • How much more information could I feed into this synthesis?
  • How “big” can I make this problem before I start losing signal?
    The prompts at this phase should ask AI to enumerate, map, and synthesize broadly. You’re building a landscape, not a recommendation. The more divergent the inputs, the better the raw material for Define.
    You now have a landscape. The natural temptation is to solve everything in it. The Define phase is about resisting that.

Define: Compress to What Matters

The job of the Define phase is to fight analysis paralysis. You have a wide landscape. Now you need to find the highest-leverage point inside it.
The “Why” questions:

  • Why is this problem (and not another) the right one to define?
  • Why do users care about it enough to change their behavior?
  • Why should the company prioritize it over competing opportunities?
    The “How can AI help” questions:
  • How many distinct problems can I collapse into one root cause?
  • How simple can I make the problem statement without losing accuracy?
  • What’s the underlying assumption I’d need to invalidate to walk away from this?
    At this phase, prompts should ask AI to rank, cluster, and surface root causes. You’re making a commitment here. AI builds the evidence base. You make the call.
    You’ve committed to a problem. Now you need to find a solution worth building. Most teams stop too early with the first idea that sounds good. The Develop phase requires more.

Develop: Generate Wildly Different Solutions

The job of the Develop phase is to fight first-solution bias. Most teams converge on the first good idea. The Random Forest approach forces divergence before convergence.
The “How can AI help” questions:

  • What would a completely different AI role (engineer, ethnographer, economist) propose as a solution?
  • How many distinct solution directions could exist?
  • What does the craziest viable solution look like?
    Prompts here should assign different expert roles to the same problem and compare outputs. The goal is genuine variety, not variations on the same theme. You want options that surprise you.
    This is also where Sam the Strategizer earns its keep. Sam is a strategic design thinking AI built specifically to stop you from jumping to a solution before you’ve fully understood the problem. You describe the problem you’re trying to solve, and Sam pushes back: reframing your constraints, surfacing unstated assumptions, suggesting research approaches you hadn’t considered.
    If you find yourself in Develop and your options all look the same, Sam is the fastest way to break out of that loop.
    You now have options that are genuinely different from each other. The job shifts from generation to judgment.

Deliver: Compress to Action

The job of the Deliver phase is to fight perfectionism. You have strong solution candidates. Now you need to ship something real, learn from it, and iterate.
The “How can AI help” questions:

  • How simple can we make the solution feel to the user?
  • How would a skeptical user evaluate this critically? What would they reject?
  • What’s the minimum viable version we could build and validate right now?
    Prompts at this phase should stress-test your solution from the user’s perspective and help you define the smallest artifact worth shipping. The goal is fast, honest validation, not a polished product that hasn’t been tested.
    Leveraging shared expertise to iterate and evaluate more divergent ideas is what separates you from AI, and from your competition. Anyone can prompt. Fewer people can judge.
    The framework works. But two things stop most teams from using it.

The Wrinkle

First: How do you know what “better” actually looks like inside your organization’s real constraints? Who decides? And how do you measure confidence in a decision when you’re making 100x more of them per day?
Second: How do non-technical team members own the process? When only engineers understand the techstack, every product decision queues up behind them. They become the bottleneck not because the work is technically complex, but because the knowledge is siloed.
Those two problems share the same root cause. The bottleneck was never technical complexity. It was always siloed knowledge.
The good news: the only bottleneck that matters to an LLM is your ability to articulate yourself. If you can describe a decision framework clearly, an LLM can apply it. If you can summarize what your system can and cannot do, a non-technical IC can use that summary to evaluate feasibility on their own.
As a proof of concept: I built a Feasibility Agent using exactly this approach. I used Human Factors methods to understand the relevant decision frameworks, summarized the required technical knowledge from Jira, and instructed an LLM to translate that knowledge into a tool anyone on the team could use. Now a product designer can assess whether a feature is technically feasible without opening a single engineering ticket.
A full breakdown of how to build your own feasibility agent is coming in a future article.
The point for now is simpler: every knowledge bottleneck in your organization is a prompt engineering problem waiting to be solved.

🧰 Choosing Your AI Tools

The framework is tool-agnostic. AI products will launch, merge, and disappear. What stays constant is the type of cognitive work each phase requires. Match your tool to the job, not the brand.

  • Discover needs breadth. You want tools that can synthesize across many sources at once: multi-document research tools, web-connected LLMs, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. The capability to look for is cross-source synthesis.
  • Define needs structure. You want tools that organize and compress information. Any general-purpose LLM handles this well. The capability to look for is analytical reasoning and ranking.
  • Develop needs variety. You want tools that generate divergent options from different angles: role-prompted LLMs, rapid prototyping tools, code generators. The capability to look for is creative generation under constraints.
  • Deliver needs speed. You want tools that reduce the friction of shipping: code generators, automation builders, prototyping environments. The capability to look for is fast artifact creation.
    You’ll likely use different tools across phases, and you may use multiple tools within a single phase. That’s fine. The prompts below are designed to work with any AI tool that accepts natural language input. Paste them wherever you do your thinking.
    One note worth adding: the way you prompt will vary by tool, and the tools themselves will keep changing. What stays constant is the underlying skill. Build the muscle memory of structured, phase-aware prompting rather than fluency with any single platform. The goal is to work well with any AI, not to master one.

What You Can Do Right Now

You do not need a technical background to start. You need curiosity and a problem worth solving.
Your ability to articulate the way you solve problems is the only real bottleneck when learning to navigate the Random Forest. The tools will change. The prompts will evolve. But the underlying skill of clear, structured thinking compounds over time regardless of what ships next quarter.
Four rules that consistently help:

  1. Protect your time. AI is fast. Undirected AI time is not. Set a scope before you start a session.
  2. Identify and update your mental model. The biggest risk isn’t bad AI output. It’s you accepting a bad output because it sounds confident. Know your domain well enough to push back.
  3. Articulate what “good enough” means. Before you prompt, define the success criterion. “I’ll know this is useful when it…” completes the thought before you start.
  4. Everyone should learn prompt engineering. Not just ICs. Managers too. The organizational literacy gap is where most of the value gets left on the table.
    And remember: the work that makes you valuable doesn’t change.
    Wherever you sit in the product process, your job is still to understand problems clearly before committing to solutions, generate options worth choosing from, and make judgment calls that a model cannot make for you. AI removes the execution tax on all of that. What you do with the freed capacity is the only question that matters.

The Double Diamond Is Dead. Long Live the Double Diamond.

Think like an agentic orchestrator.
That’s not just a mindset. It’s a practical filter. When you’re deciding whether a task belongs to you or to an AI tool, ask one question: is this a task for learning or for execution? If it requires judgment, empathy, context, or original framing, that’s yours. If it requires speed, synthesis, enumeration, or pattern-matching at scale, that’s the AI’s job. Humans learn. AI works.
The teams that win the next decade won’t be the ones with the most AI tools. They’ll be the ones who learned to think with them.
That’s a skill, not a subscription. It compounds. The practitioner who builds the habit of structured, AI-augmented reasoning today will be a different caliber of decision-maker in three years. Not because the tools got better, though they will. Because the thinking got better.
The Double Diamond didn’t become obsolete when AI arrived. It became the most important framework in your toolkit. The structure that protected you from building the wrong thing at human speed now protects you from building the wrong thing at AI speed, which is much faster and much more expensive to undo.
Your job is not to prompt better. Your job is to think better, and let the prompts carry the weight of execution.
The questions are the forcing function. The judgment is yours. That combination is the only durable competitive advantage left.

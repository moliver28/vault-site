Human Judgment Is the Edge. Stop Guarding It, Start Architecting It.

Human Judgment Is the Edge. Stop Guarding It, Start Architecting It.

Properties1
tagshuman-ai-collaboration, decision-science

Nov 14, 202624 min read

Human judgment is the last line of defense.

The field has reached consensus on this point with unusual speed. Rosala put the point bluntly: outsource your analysis to AI and you risk your credibility [1]. Sixty-nine percent of UX researchers now use AI in at least some of their projects, a 19% jump from last year [2], and the reassurance is consistent: the tools change, but human judgment remains the edge.
The instinct is sound. If you have spent years developing the ability to read between the lines of a participant interview, to sense when someone’s words diverge from their behavior, to know which finding will move a stakeholder and which will collect dust, the suggestion that a machine might replicate these capabilities feels like a category error. The capabilities are real. They are hard-won. They matter.
But the consensus misses how fast the landscape changes when you build agentic systems.


I’ve been building AI-augmented research workflows for the past year, designing systems where AI agents handle parts of the research process that used to be entirely manual. Literature review. Market analysis. Argument construction. Report synthesis. And in that work, I keep watching something happen that the “human judgment” consensus doesn’t account for: the AI makes judgment calls: not perfectly, not always well, but it makes them.
An AI agent scans hundreds of academic papers and decides which sources are most relevant to a research question. Another ranks topic opportunities by strategic fit against a scoring framework. A third synthesizes interview data into a structured report, deciding which findings deserve emphasis. Each one is a judgment call.
Whether you call these outputs “judgments” or “classifications” depends on a distinction worth unpacking. For now, notice that each one shapes a downstream decision a human used to make. The industry treats judgment as a wall between human and machine. This side is ours, that side is theirs. But the boundary keeps moving. The conversation feels stuck because we’re defending the wrong thing.
Judgment is the shared terrain of human-AI interaction, not a wall separating them. And shared terrain needs an architect.

Judgment Is Designable, Not Sacred

Here’s the uncomfortable shift. In an agentic AI workflow, someone decides which judgments stay human and which get automated. That someone is the architect: the person designing the system where human and AI judgment interact.
This is not hypothetical. It is the operational condition of any architect who has deployed an AI workflow in the past two years. Configure a pipeline that filters data before you see it, and you have made an allocation decision. Let an AI tool cluster themes from interview transcripts, and you have delegated a call that once lived in your head. These allocations are set by design. They can be changed tomorrow.

Here is why the wall metaphor breaks down. Most expert judgment is not the careful, deliberate reasoning we imagine. It is fast pattern recognition, shaped by accumulated exposure to similar situations. Cognitive science has known this for decades, and the implications for AI are more uncomfortable than the consensus admits.
Gary Klein’s Recognition-Primed Decision model found that firefighters, military commanders, and emergency physicians do not weigh options analytically. They recognize patterns from prior experience, run a rapid mental simulation, and act [3]. There is no comparison shopping. No decision matrix. Pure pattern matching, refined through thousands of hours of domain exposure.
Large language models produce outputs that look like expert intuition: pattern completion shaped by prior exposure, without the real-world experience that grounds the human version. The mechanism is different. But both shape downstream decisions. Both can be right or wrong.
The strongest version of this objection is that AI outputs lack intentionality: the machine does not “understand” what it decides, and human expertise is grounded in physical context, emotional resonance, and embodied signals that no statistical model replicates [4]. Grant the point entirely. It does not change the architectural question: who allocates which decisions, on what basis, within which system?
Kahneman’s dual-process framework sharpens the stakes. System 1 thinking (fast, automatic, intuitive) drives most expert judgment through accumulated experience. System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical) activates when System 1 encounters something unexpected [5]. The consensus treats all human judgment as System 2 work: reflective, effortful, irreplaceable by automation. Most expert judgment is System 1. Fast, automatic, experience-based. Precisely the territory where LLMs already operate at scale.
That is a design question. And design questions have answers.

The Architect’s Audit: What to Automate

The first task is auditing this territory.
Every workflow generates hundreds of judgment calls. Which participants to recruit. Which questions to prioritize in a study. How to interpret a pause in an interview. Whether a finding is strong enough to change a roadmap. Most architects never inventory these judgments explicitly. They flow through the work like water through familiar channels, invisible until something breaks.
Building an agentic AI system forces the inventory. You cannot delegate a judgment you have not identified. And identification is where most of the value lives.

The combination you do not usually see is Kahneman’s dual-process model mapped onto Endsley’s situation awareness levels from human factors research [6]. Together they produce something neither offers alone: a practical audit lens for deciding which judgments to delegate. Kahneman tells you how each judgment fires (fast intuition vs. deliberate analysis). Endsley tells you what each judgment operates on. Her three levels of environmental understanding map directly onto what you are looking for when you inventory a workflow’s judgments:
Level 1: Perception. Noticing data points, flagging patterns, categorizing inputs. Scanning forty transcripts for a specific pain point. Clustering survey responses by semantic similarity. These are real judgments that shape downstream decisions, and they operate at a scale and speed no human matches. This is where AI earns its keep.
Level 2: Comprehension. Integrating patterns into a coherent picture. Understanding why three independent usability findings share a single root cause. Recognizing that a drop-off pattern in onboarding reflects not confusion but distrust. This layer requires reading across the full landscape, not just the individual stream.
Level 3: Anticipation. Anticipating what happens next. Sensing that a stakeholder’s resistance is political rather than methodological. Recognizing when applied findings diverge from published assumptions, opening a question the field has not yet thought to ask.
The perceptual layer is the strongest candidate for automation. The comprehension and anticipation layers are where you earn your value. Not because AI cannot attempt them (it can, and increasingly does) but because these layers require the kind of contextual awareness that no training set captures in real time: organizational politics, strategic history, the subtle dynamics of a team that has been burned before.
The audit is the first act of architecture. Before you build anything, map every judgment in the workflow, classify it by cognitive level, and make a deliberate allocation decision. That decision is not binary. Sheridan and Verplank’s foundational taxonomy identified ten levels between fully manual and fully automated [25], and Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens later mapped those levels across four information-processing stages [26]. Most useful allocations live in the intermediate range, where human and machine share control in different configurations. The allocations will be wrong in places. That is expected. They exist to be revised. But they must exist before you touch a single prompt.

Designing the Human-AI Judgment Architecture

Knowing what to automate is necessary. It is not sufficient. The design of the system determines whether automated judgments produce reliable outputs or confident errors.
Most architects who build AI workflows start by delegating tasks: “summarize this transcript,” “extract the key themes,” “draft an affinity map.” The outputs feel useful. The efficiency gains are real. But there is a gap between delegating a task and delegating a judgment that most workflows obscure.
A task has defined inputs and outputs. A judgment has context. When you ask an AI agent to cluster interview themes, the output depends entirely on what context you provide: the research questions, the participant demographics, the product domain, the strategic priorities the study was designed to inform. Strip the context and the clustering is technically competent but strategically useless. The same data, framed by different context, produces different and legitimate clusterings.
Edwin Hutchins observed something essential while studying navigation teams on naval vessels [7]. His central finding reframes the entire picture: cognition does not live in any single person’s head. It is distributed across people, instruments, charts, and procedures. The system thinks. The quality of the system’s thinking depends on how those elements are connected, what information flows between them, and what each element can see.
An agentic AI workflow is exactly this kind of distributed cognitive system. The AI agents, the data they process, the prompts that shape them, the human who reviews their outputs: all nodes in a shared cognitive architecture. The quality of judgment depends not on the capability of any single node but on how the whole thing fits together.

This is why architecture matters more than capability. “Can the AI cluster themes?” Of course it can. The real issue is whether you have designed the joint system so the AI’s calls are grounded in the right context, constrained by the right boundaries, and connected to the right downstream decisions?
Most AI workflows never ask that question. Donella Meadows mapped where interventions in complex systems actually produce change [8]. Her hierarchy shows where small changes produce big effects: the most common interventions (tweaking parameters, adjusting prompts, swapping models) rank at the bottom. The highest-leverage interventions change a system’s information flows, its rules, and its goals. Each of the three principles below operates at a higher leverage point than task delegation alone.

Context architecture

Every AI agent in the system needs a defined context window: what information it can see, what it cannot, and why. Think of it as need-to-know access. An agent tasked with identifying usability issues in a transcript needs the task flows being tested and the participant’s experience level. It does not need the product roadmap. An agent synthesizing across multiple studies needs the strategic questions driving the research program. The agent sees exactly what it needs to make its judgment, nothing more.
In practice, my literature review agent receives the research question, the target domains, and the inclusion criteria. It does not receive my content calendar, my audience analytics, or my competitive positioning. Each boundary is a deliberate judgment allocation decision. Designing context architecture changes the structure of what the system can know, a fundamentally higher-leverage intervention than adjusting any individual prompt.

Escalation paths

Not every judgment stays where you put it. A well-designed architecture includes clear triggers for surfacing an AI-allocated judgment to human review. Confidence thresholds. Contradiction detection. Novel patterns that fall outside what the model has seen before. The escalation path is the override mechanism that activates when the fast system encounters something it was not built for.
Leveson’s STAMP framework makes the principle concrete: system failures trace to breakdowns in control, not chains of broken components [9]. An agentic AI workflow without escalation paths is a control system without a safety structure. The AI will encounter something outside its scope. The only question is whether the human-in-the-loop architecture routes it to a human before the error compounds. The design challenge is clear boundaries between judgment zones, with triggers simple and rare enough to be taken seriously when they fire.
I trigger escalation when an agent’s output contradicts its own prior classification on similar data, or when it encounters a source that matches none of its existing categorization criteria. These triggers are deliberately rare: two to three escalations per literature review cycle, not twenty.

Feedback loops

I structure this as a decision log the agent generates alongside every output: what it decided, which context it weighted most heavily, and its confidence level. Reviewing ten decision logs takes five minutes. Reviewing ten raw outputs takes an hour. The log is the feedback loop, the simplest form of AI transparency in practice.
The principle behind it is straightforward. The sensemaking research is clear [10]: making sense of ambiguous situations requires information that is interpretable and reviewable, not buried in logs. Meadows identified the strength of feedback loops as one of the most powerful leverage points in any system [8]. A feedback loop that is too slow, too noisy, or disconnected from the decisions it should inform is functionally the same as no feedback at all. Can the human architect see what the system decided, on what basis, and what happened as a result? If yes, the feedback loop is working. If not, the system is flying blind.

The systems I have built that work share a common trait: they were designed around judgment, not around tasks.
The pipeline that scores and ranks blog topics against live market conversations works because the scoring criteria are explicitly defined, the context window includes the content strategy and academic literature base, the escalation path surfaces anything ambiguous, and the feedback loop lets me see what was weighted and why.
The pipeline that failed was the one I built around a task (“summarize these sources”) without designing the judgment architecture around it.
The difference between a useful AI workflow and a dangerous one is rarely capability. It is the presence or absence of deliberate architecture around context, escalation, and feedback.

The Architect’s Paradox: Review and Control

The architecture ships. The system runs. And here the hardest work begins: maintaining human oversight of a system designed to operate without you.
The AI trust research is unambiguous on one point: calibration is everything. A single automation failure can produce distrust that sticks, even after the system returns to high reliability [16]. Lee and See’s foundational framework showed how this works: when people trust a system more than it deserves, or less than it deserves, they stop using it well [11]. One bad call in a high-stakes moment erases the credibility built by a hundred good calls in routine ones. Your review process must catch miscalibrations before they reach high-stakes decisions, not after.
I have watched this fail firsthand. An agent I configured to score market opportunities performed well for months. Then my content direction shifted. The agent kept scoring by the old criteria. The outputs looked clean. They were not. The terrain shifted and the architecture did not notice.
This creates a risk that runs in two directions. The risk to you: when the AI makes a judgment calibrated to old conditions, you act on outdated ground truth. An AI agent flags a compliance workflow as routine because it matched historical patterns, but new regulations have made it high-stakes. The risk to the system: that failure does not erode confidence in one judgment. It erodes trust in the architecture’s capacity to judge at all [11].

The complacency literature deepens the problem for architects specifically. Operators who rely on automated systems show measurable declines in monitoring and detection performance [12]. In cognitive automation specifically, expert workers experience gradual, invisible skill erosion in the very tasks they delegated [13]. When the system handles the work reliably, you stop practicing the skill. When it eventually fails, you lack the capacity to catch it. Stop reviewing the territory and you lose the ability to see when conditions shift.
And here the deepest tension in agentic work surfaces. Chris Argyris and Donald Schön drew the distinction between single-loop learning (“Did we do the thing right?”) and double-loop learning (“Are we doing the right thing?”) [14]. Most review processes are single-loop: check the AI’s output for accuracy, fix errors, move on. An architect who reviews AI-clustered themes for accuracy is doing single-loop work. One who reads the same clusters for structure, asking “What does the clustering pattern reveal about how participants organize their experience?”, is doing double-loop work. Same output. Different question. Your review process must be double-loop.
But the orchestration paradox makes this difficult. If you spend your cognitive energy managing the system (checking outputs, troubleshooting failures, adjusting prompts), you are not spending it learning from the system’s outputs. The tasks the system demands of its human are frequently the opposite of the tasks that make the human valuable.
Willpower will not solve it. Architecture will.
The agentic ecosystem has already demonstrated why. Through deployment failures and operational surprises, the industry has arrived at the same structural requirements that cognitive systems engineering identified decades ago through theory. In 2002, Christoffersen and Woods studied coordination breakdowns across aviation, process control, and military command [17]. Every failure traced to two missing properties: observability (the human could not see what the automation decided or why) and directability (the human could not redirect it when circumstances changed). In March 2026, the agentic AI industry arrived at the identical insight independently. Kyndryl named “agentic drift” as the hidden risk of deploying AI at scale [18]. CIO Magazine reported that agentic systems “don’t fail suddenly; they drift over time” [19]. IBM launched “agentic observability” as a new infrastructure category [20]. McKinsey reframed the foundational question: “Agency isn’t a feature. It’s a transfer of decision rights” [21].
The engineering teams are building monitoring tools. The management consultancies are naming organizational risks. Neither conversation has the design framework that connects them. Three commitments provide that framework. Each is grounded in principles that predate AI. Each is validated by the deployment patterns emerging now.

Design for visibility of judgements

Woods developed the theory of graceful extensibility to explain why some systems absorb surprises while others shatter [22]. The core insight: resilient systems do not just detect failure. They maintain reserve capacity for maneuver: slack between normal operation and the boundary where things break. The question is not whether a judgment allocation will encounter surprise, but whether the system has room to absorb it. For each judgment allocation in the architecture, define three conditions: the operating condition (where it works), the boundary condition (where it breaks), and the reserve condition (what slack exists between current operation and the boundary). Three questions per node: Under what conditions does this allocation work? Under what conditions does it fail? How close are we to the failure condition, and what buffer exists?
Then build the controls. Every agent in the architecture must satisfy two structural requirements. Observability: the architect can see what the agent decided, what context it had access to, what it weighted, and what was invisible to it. Directability: the architect can redirect any agent’s priorities, constraints, or context window without rebuilding the pipeline. The decision log I described earlier is observability. What most agentic workflows lack is directability: the ability to intervene mid-pipeline, not just review after the fact. If you can only audit an agent’s output but cannot change its operating parameters in response, you are monitoring, not commanding. The observability tools now shipping from major platforms build this infrastructure. Your job is to ensure these properties are designed into the judgment allocation itself, not bolted on afterward.
Design detection signals at multiple levels: data distribution changes (perceptual), pattern shifts in agent behavior (comprehension), and strategic misalignment between outputs and goals (projection). I design my detection signals around categories of shift (changes in data distribution, changes in agent behavior patterns, changes in strategic alignment) rather than specific signals. Categories hold as capabilities expand. Specific signals age out with the next model update.

Build oversight that sharpens the overseer

Skill maintenance cannot be a personal discipline. It must be a structural property of the workflow. The difference: “exercise regularly” versus “design your commute so that walking is the only option.”
Instead of reviewing AI-clustered themes for accuracy (single-loop, skill-atrophying), structure the workflow to require independent analysis of a sample before viewing the AI’s output, then compare (double-loop, skill-maintaining). You cannot complete the second version without exercising the judgment it preserves. You do not need willpower. The workflow demands it.
Prioritize by cognitive level. Projection skills (anticipating consequences, sensing organizational dynamics, recognizing strategic misalignment) degrade fastest and matter most. They depend on accumulated contextual awareness that no refresher quickly restores. Comprehension skills (integrating patterns into a coherent picture) degrade more slowly. Perception skills (noticing data, flagging patterns) are the easiest to offload and quickest to recover. Weight your maintenance investment toward projection. That is where your irreplaceable value lives.

Audit for drift, not just defects

Rasmussen studied how complex systems (nuclear plants, hospitals, transportation networks) behave under real operating pressure [23]. He found that every system drifts toward the boundary of acceptable performance when efficiency pressure mounts. In agentic architectures, this takes a specific form: you delegate a judgment. It works. Confidence grows. Adjacent judgments get informally delegated (an expanded prompt here, a loosened constraint there) without updating the architecture. I have seen this firsthand: a scoring agent that started with three judgment types quietly absorbed five because each expansion worked and nobody logged the change. Over time, the documented allocation diverges from the actual allocation. The system looks the same on paper. It is not the same system. Kyndryl’s analysis found that “AI agents can appear reliable while working toward unwanted outcomes” [18]. CIO Magazine documented the pattern: degradation is quiet, and “by the time the failure is visible, the risk has often been accumulating for months” [19].
The practical mechanism: maintain a live allocation map that records which judgments are human, which are automated, and which are shared. Schedule reviews with the same rigor as sprint retrospectives, but also trigger them based on three event types: repeated escalation patterns (the system surfaces the same ambiguity type more than twice in a cycle), capability step-changes (a model update, a new agent, a context window expansion), and drift signals (the live allocation map has diverged from the designed allocation).

For the audit itself, five questions adapted from cognitive work analysis (a method for analyzing how work actually gets done in complex environments, developed by Vicente [24]) provide the structure:

  1. Constraints: What has changed in the environment, the tools, or the stakeholder expectations?
  2. Allocations: Are the judgment allocations still appropriate given current constraints?
  3. Strategies: Are there new approaches the system should support that the current architecture does not?
  4. Undocumented shifts: Has the human-AI division drifted from the designed allocation without deliberate redesign?
  5. Competence: Has the architect’s practiced judgment kept pace with the system’s expanded scope?
    Prioritize the highest-leverage allocations first: where downstream consequences of error are greatest, where the AI’s context is most constrained, or where the terrain has shifted most since the last review. Without a method, audits become ceremonial. Without prioritization, they become infeasible as the system scales.
    Design for these three properties and you stay in command. Neglect them and you become a custodian of a system you no longer fully understand.

The Expanding Question

Three studies landed on my desk in the same week last month. Different researchers, different products, different lifecycle stages. One found usability friction in an AI feature. Another documented adoption hesitation among power users. The third tracked a drop-off pattern in onboarding. Solid work, each of them. Clear recommendations. Nothing that would move a roadmap on its own.
I happened to be reading all three at once. A pattern surfaced that none of them reported. Users were not resisting our AI features because of usability problems. They were resisting because they could not figure out whether the AI was worth the risk of trusting. The question behind every finding was identical: not “Can I use this?” but “Should I bet on this?”
No single study asked that question. No AI agent, working from one data stream, would have connected those three signals. I saw it because I was sitting at the intersection of all three, doing the projection-level work the architecture had preserved my capacity to do.
That insight changed everything downstream. Instead of three follow-up studies, I launched six systematic literature reviews across trust in automation, psychometric best practices, and economic value modeling. I built a new taxonomy connecting AI readiness, adoption barriers, and risk tolerance to our existing jobs-to-be-done framework. From a single insight, two deliverables: an executive report grounding the strategic case in economics and a product-engineering-design report translating the findings into tactical priorities. Three isolated studies became the foundation for our enterprise AI adoption research strategy.
That kind of synthesis only happens when the architect still walks the terrain.
The better question is architectural. It applies to every architect who designs systems where humans and AI share judgment. Which decisions need what level of human involvement, in which circumstances, within which systems? The question never settles. But it has a design practice. Build visibility and command so you can see and steer the system. Structure oversight that sharpens your judgment rather than letting it atrophy. Audit for drift, catching the slow divergence between what you designed and what you are actually running.
These three commitments reinforce each other. Visibility reveals where oversight needs strengthening. Active oversight maintains the judgment that makes audits meaningful. Audits surface the drift signals that update the visibility architecture. Each cycle sharpens the next.
The tech industry has spent two years drawing boundaries. What can humans do that AI cannot? What is ours to protect? Boundaries are lines on a map. The terrain does not hold still for cartographers.
Where to draw the line was never the right question. The right question is who designs the system. Who builds the architecture where every agent, human and AI, makes better decisions because of how the whole thing fits together?
That is the question worth protecting.
